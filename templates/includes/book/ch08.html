Chapter 8: The qubit

Big idea. A bit is 0 or 1. A qubit is a unit arrow \( \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle \) that can lean towards both. The two complex numbers \( \alpha \) and \( \beta \) hold the whole state. A picture called the Bloch sphere shows the arrow as a point on a ball.

Bits

Everything in an ordinary computer is bits. A bit is a switch: off or on, 0 or 1. Eight bits make a byte, and a byte can be any of \( 2^8 = 256 \) patterns, but at any moment it is exactly one of them.

Qubits

A qubit (quantum bit) is a physical thing with two basic states, which we name \( |0\rangle \) and \( |1\rangle \). It could be an atom in its lowest or next-lowest energy level, a photon polarised up or sideways, or a tiny electric current going one way or the other. The hardware is not our concern. What matters is the rule from Part I: the state of the qubit is a unit vector

\[ |\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle, \qquad |\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1 \]

with \( \alpha \) and \( \beta \) complex. When \( \beta = 0 \) the qubit is simply \( |0\rangle \), a plain bit. When \( \alpha = 0 \) it is \( |1\rangle \). In between, it is a mix, and the word for a mix is superposition.

Superposition does not mean "it is secretly 0 or 1 and we don't know which". It means the arrow genuinely points between them, and both parts can interfere with each other, as in Chapter 6. Only when you look (Chapter 9) does it become 0 or 1.

These come up constantly. Each one is a unit vector; check one or two yourself.

Notice that plus, minus and plus-i all give a 50/50 chance of 0 or 1. If you only ever measured them, you could not tell them apart. Yet they are different arrows, and gates treat them differently. The difference is entirely in the phase, the angle between \( \alpha \) and \( \beta \). Phase is invisible to a single measurement and essential to everything else.

A phase you can ignore, and one you cannot

Multiply the whole state by a unit complex number, say \( |\psi'\rangle = e^{i\theta}|\psi\rangle \). Every amplitude turns by the same angle, so every \( |\alpha|^2 \) is unchanged, and so is every interference term (the angle difference is what matters). The states \( |\psi\rangle \) and \( -|\psi\rangle \) and \( i|\psi\rangle \) are physically the same. This is called a global phase, and you may drop it.

But turning only \( \beta \), as in \( \alpha|0\rangle + e^{i\theta}\beta|1\rangle \), changes the angle between the two parts. That is a relative phase, and it is real. The difference between \( |+\rangle \) and \( |-\rangle \) is a relative phase of \( \pi \).

The Bloch sphere

Two complex numbers are four real numbers. The length rule removes one, and the global phase removes another. Two real numbers are left, and two numbers can be drawn as a point on the surface of a ball. That drawing is the Bloch sphere.

Figure 8.1: The Bloch sphere. North pole \( |0\rangle \), south pole \( |1\rangle \), equator for equal mixes.

New axes. The \( x \), \( y \), \( z \) of the Bloch sphere are not the \( \hat{x} \) and \( \hat{y} \) of Chapter 2. There, \( |0\rangle \) and \( |1\rangle \) were two arrows at right angles on a flat page. Here they are the north and south poles of a ball, on the \( z \) axis. The ball is a different picture of the same two numbers \( \alpha, \beta \), chosen so that every qubit is one point on its surface.

Any qubit can be written with two angles, \( \theta \) (down from the north pole) and \( \phi \) (around the equator):

\[ |\psi\rangle = \cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}\,|0\rangle + e^{i\phi}\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\,|1\rangle \]
  • \( \theta = 0 \): \( \cos 0 = 1 \), \( \sin 0 = 0 \), so the state is \( |0\rangle \). The north pole.
  • \( \theta = \pi \): the state is \( |1\rangle \). The south pole.
  • \( \theta = \pi/2, \phi = 0 \): \( \cos\tfrac{\pi}{4} = \sin\tfrac{\pi}{4} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \), so the state is \( |+\rangle \). On the equator, pointing along \( +x \).
  • \( \theta = \pi/2, \phi = \pi \): \( e^{i\pi} = -1 \), so \( |-\rangle \). Equator, along \( -x \).
  • \( \theta = \pi/2, \phi = \pi/2 \): \( e^{i\pi/2} = i \), so \( |{+i}\rangle \). Equator, along \( +y \).

The chance of measuring 0 is \( \cos^2\tfrac{\theta}{2} \): 1 at the north pole, \( \tfrac{1}{2} \) on the equator, 0 at the south pole. The angle \( \phi \) is the relative phase. Moving around the equator changes the phase and nothing about the 0/1 chances.

The halves in \( \theta/2 \) are not a typo. States that are opposite on the Bloch sphere, like \( |0\rangle \) and \( |1\rangle \), are orthogonal as vectors (90 degrees apart in Chapter 2's sense) but 180 degrees apart on the ball. The ball doubles all angles.

Why one qubit already beats one bit

You cannot read \( \alpha \) and \( \beta \) out of a single qubit; measurement gives you one bit, 0 or 1, and destroys the rest. So a qubit does not store "more information" in the everyday sense. What it gives you is the ability to put a value into a superposition, apply gates that act on both parts at once, and arrange interference so that the answer you want is the one you see. That is a different kind of computing, and the next chapters build it up piece by piece.

Try it. Open the Bloch Sphere Simulator. The arrow starts at the north pole, \( |0\rangle \). Press H: the arrow drops to the equator, \( |+\rangle \). Press S: it slides a quarter turn around the equator to \( |{+i}\rangle \). Press S again: \( |-\rangle \). Watch the probabilities shown on the page: on the equator they stay 50/50 no matter how you turn.
Check yourself

Q1. Is \( \tfrac{1}{2}|0\rangle + \tfrac{1}{2}|1\rangle \) a valid qubit?

A1. No. \( \tfrac{1}{4} + \tfrac{1}{4} = \tfrac{1}{2} \neq 1 \). Divide by its length \( \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \) to normalise it, and you get \( |+\rangle \).

Q2. Are \( |+\rangle \) and \( -|+\rangle \) different qubits?

A2. No. The \( -1 \) is a global phase. Both are the same point on the Bloch sphere.

Q3. Where on the Bloch sphere is \( \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle - i|1\rangle) \)?

A3. On the equator along \( -y \): \( \theta = \pi/2 \), \( \phi = 3\pi/2 \) (since \( e^{i3\pi/2} = -i \)).
