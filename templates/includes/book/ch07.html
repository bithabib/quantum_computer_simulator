Chapter 7: The language of kets and bras

Big idea. Physicists write arrows as \( |\psi\rangle \) and a flipped-over, conjugated copy as \( \langle\psi| \). Putting them together, \( \langle\phi|\psi\rangle \), is the dot product, and it gives an amplitude. This chapter collects Part I into one notation you will use for the rest of the book.

Kets are column vectors

A ket \( |\psi\rangle \) is a column vector with complex entries. (The Greek letter \( \psi \), "psi", is traditional for a quantum state; it is just a name.) For a qubit:

\[ |\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle = \begin{pmatrix} \alpha \\ \beta \end{pmatrix} \]

Bras are flipped, conjugated rows

A bra \( \langle\psi| \) is what you get by turning the column into a row and taking the complex conjugate of every entry:

\[ \langle\psi| = \begin{pmatrix} \alpha^* & \beta^* \end{pmatrix} \]

The word is a joke: bra + ket = bracket. The operation (transpose, then conjugate) is called the dagger, written \( \dagger \). So \( \langle\psi| = (|\psi\rangle)^\dagger \).

Bra-ket is the dot product

Put a bra (row) in front of a ket (column) and multiply by the row-times-column rule from Chapter 3:

\[ \langle\phi|\psi\rangle = \begin{pmatrix} \phi_0^* & \phi_1^* \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \psi_0 \\ \psi_1 \end{pmatrix} = \phi_0^*\psi_0 + \phi_1^*\psi_1 \]

This is the dot product of Chapter 2, with one upgrade: the left entries are conjugated. The upgrade makes a state's dot product with itself come out real and positive:

\[ \langle\psi|\psi\rangle = \alpha^*\alpha + \beta^*\beta = |\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1 \]

That is the normalisation rule again. A valid quantum state has \( \langle\psi|\psi\rangle = 1 \). This is called the inner product; it is the dot product's proper name when the entries can be complex.

Inner products are amplitudes

Here is the payoff. Take the inner product of a basis state with your state:

\[ \langle 0|\psi\rangle = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} \alpha \\ \beta \end{pmatrix} = \alpha, \qquad \langle 1|\psi\rangle = \beta \]

The inner product pulls out the amplitude. So the Born rule from Chapter 6 can be written in one line that works for any state and any outcome:

The probability of finding \( |\psi\rangle \) in the state \( |\phi\rangle \) is \( P = |\langle\phi|\psi\rangle|^2 \).

In words: "how much does \( \psi \) agree with \( \phi \), squared". That is the dot product's old meaning from Chapter 2, now doing physics.

Orthonormal bases in bra-ket

The two basis states are at right angles and have length 1:

\[ \langle 0|0\rangle = 1, \quad \langle 1|1\rangle = 1, \quad \langle 0|1\rangle = 0, \quad \langle 1|0\rangle = 0 \]

Physicists shorten these four lines to \( \langle i|j\rangle = \delta_{ij} \), where \( \delta_{ij} \) (the Kronecker delta) is 1 when \( i = j \) and 0 otherwise. It is nothing deeper than "same: 1, different: 0".

The plus and minus states from Chapter 3 are another orthonormal basis. Check:

\[ \langle +|-\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} = \tfrac{1}{2}(1 - 1) = 0 \]

Same arrow, different basis, different components. Measuring in the \( \{|0\rangle, |1\rangle\} \) basis and measuring in the \( \{|+\rangle, |-\rangle\} \) basis ask different questions of the same qubit.

The dagger of a matrix

Apply the dagger to a matrix the same way: transpose, then conjugate every entry.

\[ M = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} \quad\Rightarrow\quad M^\dagger = \begin{pmatrix} a^* & c^* \\ b^* & d^* \end{pmatrix} \]

A matrix is unitary if its dagger is its inverse: \( U^\dagger U = I \). Unitary matrices keep inner products, and so keep lengths, and so keep probabilities adding to 1. Every quantum gate is unitary, and \( U^\dagger \) is how you undo it. For the gates \( X \) and \( Z \), all entries are real and the matrices are symmetric, so \( X^\dagger = X \) and \( Z^\dagger = Z \), which matches \( XX = I \) from Chapter 3.

One worked example with a complex entry, the S gate from Chapter 5:

\[ S = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & i \end{pmatrix}, \qquad S^\dagger = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -i \end{pmatrix}, \qquad S^\dagger S = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & (-i)(i) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = I \]

Ket times bra: building machines from arrows

Write a ket before a bra and you get a column times a row, which is a matrix:

\[ |0\rangle\langle 0| = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad |1\rangle\langle 1| = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \]

These are projectors: \( |0\rangle\langle 0| \) keeps the \( |0\rangle \) part of an arrow and throws the rest away. You can now write the gates from Chapter 3 without any grids at all:

\[ I = |0\rangle\langle 0| + |1\rangle\langle 1|, \qquad Z = |0\rangle\langle 0| - |1\rangle\langle 1|, \qquad X = |0\rangle\langle 1| + |1\rangle\langle 0| \]

Read \( X \) as "send 1 to 0, and send 0 to 1". This way of writing matrices is how physicists think about gates, and it will make Chapter 11's two-qubit gates easy to write down.

Check yourself

Q1. If \( |\psi\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle + i|1\rangle) \), write \( \langle\psi| \).

A1. \( \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -i \end{pmatrix} \). The \( i \) became \( -i \).

Q2. Compute \( \langle\psi|\psi\rangle \) for that state.

A2. \( \tfrac{1}{2}(1 \cdot 1 + (-i)(i)) = \tfrac{1}{2}(1 + 1) = 1 \). Normalised.

Q3. What is the probability of finding \( |+\rangle \) in the state \( |0\rangle \)?

A3. \( |\langle 0|+\rangle|^2 = |\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|^2 = \tfrac{1}{2} \).
