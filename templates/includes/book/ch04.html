Chapter 4: How fast things change (derivatives)

Big idea. A derivative is a speed. It tells you how fast one thing changes when another thing changes. Quantum physics has one master equation, and it says how fast the state arrow turns.

Speed is a derivative

A car's position changes with time. Its speed is "how much the position changes per second". If the car is at 10 metres at time 2 s and 16 metres at time 3 s, it moved 6 metres in 1 second, so its speed was about 6 m/s.

\[ \text{speed} = \frac{\text{change in position}}{\text{change in time}} = \frac{16 - 10}{3 - 2} = 6 \]

But "about" is not good enough if the car is speeding up. The true speed at exactly 2 s is what you get when you make the time gap smaller and smaller. That limit is the derivative of position with respect to time, written \( \frac{dx}{dt} \). The letter \( d \) just means "a tiny change in".

Slope is a derivative

Draw position against time on a graph. The derivative at a point is the slope of the graph there: how steep it is. Steep means fast. Flat means stopped. Sloping down means going backwards.

You can find derivatives of simple formulas with rules. The one rule you need for this book:

\[ \text{if } x = t^n \text{ then } \frac{dx}{dt} = n\,t^{\,n-1} \]

Examples: the derivative of \( t^2 \) is \( 2t \). The derivative of \( t^3 \) is \( 3t^2 \). The derivative of \( 5t \) is \( 5 \) (a straight line has a constant slope). The derivative of a constant like \( 7 \) is \( 0 \) (a flat line has no slope).

Try it on the car. Say its position is \( x = t^2 \) metres. Then its speed is \( \frac{dx}{dt} = 2t \). At 2 s the speed is exactly 4 m/s, at 3 s it is 6 m/s. The car is speeding up.

The derivative of a derivative

Speeding up has a name: acceleration. It is "how fast the speed changes", which is the derivative of the speed, which is the derivative of the derivative of position. We write it \( \frac{d^2x}{dt^2} \) and call it the second derivative. For \( x = t^2 \), the speed is \( 2t \) and the acceleration is \( 2 \): constant. This car gains 2 m/s every second.

The second derivative also tells you the shape of a graph. Positive means the curve bends upward like a smile. Negative means it bends downward like a frown. Where the first derivative is zero (flat) and the second is negative, you are at the top of a hill, a maximum. Where it is zero and the second is positive, you are at the bottom of a valley, a minimum.

Where does quantum physics use this?

Everywhere, but you only need to know two places.

1. The master equation. In Chapter 1 we said a quantum state is an arrow and things that happen are machines that turn the arrow. Erwin Schrödinger wrote the equation that says how fast the arrow turns as time passes:

\[ i\hbar \frac{d}{dt}|\psi\rangle = H|\psi\rangle \]

Read it like this: "the rate of change of the state arrow (left side) equals a certain machine \( H \), called the Hamiltonian, applied to the arrow (right side)". \( \hbar \) is a tiny fixed number from nature, and \( i \) is the number you meet in the next chapter. That is the whole law of motion of the quantum world. Every gate in a quantum computer is really this equation run for a short time with a chosen \( H \). We will not solve it in this book, but now you can read it.

2. Learning. Modern quantum computers are often "trained" like a neural network. You have a circuit with some adjustable angles, a score that says how well it is doing, and you adjust the angles to improve the score. How do you know which way to turn each angle? Take the derivative of the score with respect to the angle. Positive derivative: turning the angle up raises the score. Zero derivative everywhere: the score is flat and you are stuck. (That "flat" problem has a name, a barren plateau, and it is what the research on this site is about.)

Integrals, in one paragraph

A derivative takes you from position to speed. The reverse trip, from speed back to position, is an integral: add up speed times tiny time steps to get total distance. The integral of \( 2t \) is \( t^2 \) (plus a constant for where you started). On a graph it is the area under the curve. Chapter 6 mentions them once, where a probability is spread over a line instead of a few points, but in this book it is enough to know they undo derivatives.

Check yourself

Q1. If \( x = t^3 \), what is the speed at \( t = 2 \)?

A1. \( \frac{dx}{dt} = 3t^2 = 3 \times 4 = 12 \).

Q2. What is the acceleration at \( t = 2 \)?

A2. The derivative of \( 3t^2 \) is \( 6t = 12 \).

Q3. In the Schrödinger equation, which side is "how fast the state changes"?

A3. The left side, \( \frac{d}{dt}|\psi\rangle \).
