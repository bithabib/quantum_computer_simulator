Chapter 6: Chance and amplitude (probability)

Big idea. Ordinary chance is a number between 0 and 1. Quantum chance comes from a complex number called an amplitude, and the chance is its length squared. Because amplitudes have angles, two ways of reaching the same result can add up or cancel out. That cancelling is called interference, and it is the engine of every quantum algorithm.

Ordinary probability

Flip a fair coin. The chance of heads is \( \tfrac{1}{2} \). Roll a die. The chance of a six is \( \tfrac{1}{6} \). A probability is a number between 0 (never) and 1 (always), and the probabilities of all the possible outcomes add up to 1. Two rules cover almost everything:

  • Either-or: add. The chance of rolling a 5 or a 6 is \( \tfrac{1}{6} + \tfrac{1}{6} = \tfrac{1}{3} \).
  • Both: multiply. The chance of heads and then a six is \( \tfrac{1}{2} \times \tfrac{1}{6} = \tfrac{1}{12} \).

If you flip a coin 1000 times you expect about 500 heads. Not exactly 500, but close, and closer (as a fraction) the more you flip. Probability tells you about the long run, never about a single flip.

A quantum coin

Now the twist. In Chapter 2 we met the qubit \( 0.6|0\rangle + 0.8|1\rangle \). Here is the rule for what happens when you look at it:

The Born rule. If a state is \( \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle \), then looking at it gives 0 with probability \( |\alpha|^2 \) and 1 with probability \( |\beta|^2 \).

For our qubit: chance of 0 is \( 0.6^2 = 0.36 \), chance of 1 is \( 0.8^2 = 0.64 \). They add to 1, exactly because the arrow has length 1. That is why quantum states must be unit vectors: the lengths squared are the probabilities, and probabilities must add to 1.

The numbers \( \alpha \) and \( \beta \) are called probability amplitudes, or just amplitudes. They are not probabilities. They are complex numbers whose length squared is a probability. Since \( |z|^2 = z z^* \) from Chapter 5, you compute a probability from an amplitude by multiplying it by its conjugate.

Why amplitudes and not just probabilities?

Because amplitudes have angles, and probabilities do not. Here is where everything changes.

Suppose a particle can reach a detector by two paths, A and B. In the ordinary world, the chance of arriving is the chance via A plus the chance via B. More paths, more chance. Always.

In the quantum world, each path has an amplitude, say \( \alpha_1 \) for A and \( \alpha_2 \) for B. The rule is: add the amplitudes first, then square.

\[ P = |\alpha_1 + \alpha_2|^2 \]

Let us work it out using the conjugate trick. Expand it like \( (x + y)(x + y) \):

\[ |\alpha_1 + \alpha_2|^2 = (\alpha_1 + \alpha_2)(\alpha_1 + \alpha_2)^* = (\alpha_1 + \alpha_2)(\alpha_1^* + \alpha_2^*) \] \[ = \alpha_1\alpha_1^* + \alpha_2\alpha_2^* + \alpha_1\alpha_2^* + \alpha_2\alpha_1^* \] \[ = |\alpha_1|^2 + |\alpha_2|^2 + (\alpha_1\alpha_2^* + \alpha_2\alpha_1^*) \]

The first two terms are the ordinary "chance via A plus chance via B". The bracket is new. Write each amplitude in length-and-angle form, \( \alpha_1 = r_1 e^{i\phi_1} \) and \( \alpha_2 = r_2 e^{i\phi_2} \). Then \( \alpha_1\alpha_2^* = r_1 r_2 e^{i(\phi_1 - \phi_2)} \) and \( \alpha_2\alpha_1^* \) is its conjugate, \( r_1 r_2 e^{-i(\phi_1 - \phi_2)} \). A number plus its conjugate is twice the real part, and the real part of \( e^{i\theta} \) is \( \cos\theta \). So the bracket is \( 2 r_1 r_2 \cos(\phi_1 - \phi_2) \), and:

\[ P = |\alpha_1|^2 + |\alpha_2|^2 + 2|\alpha_1||\alpha_2|\cos(\phi_1 - \phi_2) \]

The last term is the interference term. It depends on the difference in angle between the two paths:

  • Same angle (\( \phi_1 = \phi_2 \)): \( \cos 0 = 1 \), the term is positive, the paths help each other. Constructive interference. The chance is bigger than the sum.
  • Opposite angles (\( \phi_1 - \phi_2 = \pi \)): \( \cos\pi = -1 \), the term is negative. Destructive interference. If the lengths are equal the chance is exactly zero: two open paths and the particle never arrives.

Try it with numbers. Let \( \alpha_1 = \tfrac{1}{2} \) and \( \alpha_2 = \tfrac{1}{2} \). Then \( P = |\tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{2}|^2 = 1 \). Now let \( \alpha_2 = -\tfrac{1}{2} \) (same length, angle \( \pi \)). Then \( P = |\tfrac{1}{2} - \tfrac{1}{2}|^2 = 0 \). Each path alone would give \( \tfrac{1}{4} \). Together they give 1 or 0 depending only on a sign. No ordinary probability does that.

This is the trick behind every quantum algorithm. Arrange the amplitudes so that wrong answers cancel out and the right answer adds up. Then when you look, you see the right answer.

The two rules for amplitudes

Just like ordinary probability has "add" and "multiply", amplitudes do too:

Figure 6.1: Either path A or path B to the same place: add the amplitudes, \( \alpha = \alpha_1 + \alpha_2 \).

Figure 6.2: First step A then step B, one after the other: multiply the amplitudes, \( \alpha = \alpha_1 \alpha_2 \).

And at the very end, when you look, probability equals length squared. Three rules. They are the whole of quantum mechanics as far as this book is concerned.

\[ P = |\alpha|^2, \qquad \alpha_{\text{either}} = \alpha_1 + \alpha_2, \qquad \alpha_{\text{then}} = \alpha_1\alpha_2 \]

Why "look" matters

Interference only happens if you do not know which path was taken. If you put a detector on path A, the two paths no longer add as amplitudes; they add as ordinary probabilities, and the cancellation disappears. Looking changes the result. This is not a limit of our instruments. It is how the world works, and Chapter 9 turns it into a precise rule for measuring qubits.

Many outcomes

A die has six outcomes. A quantum "die" would be a six-entry arrow \( (\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_6) \) with \( |\alpha_1|^2 + \dots + |\alpha_6|^2 = 1 \). Two qubits have four outcomes (00, 01, 10, 11), so a four-entry arrow. Ten qubits have 1024. The arrow gets long, but the rule stays the same: the length squared of each entry is the chance of that outcome. (If the outcomes form a continuous line, like a position, the sum becomes the integral from Chapter 4. We will not need that.)

Check yourself

Q1. A qubit is \( \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|0\rangle + \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|1\rangle \). Chance of 0?

A1. \( (1/\sqrt{2})^2 = \tfrac{1}{2} \). A fair quantum coin.

Q2. A qubit is \( \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|0\rangle - \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|1\rangle \). Chance of 1?

A2. Also \( \tfrac{1}{2} \). The minus sign does not change the length squared. But it will change what happens when a gate is applied: see Chapter 10.

Q3. Two paths have amplitudes \( \tfrac{1}{2} \) and \( \tfrac{i}{2} \). Chance of arriving?

A3. \( |\tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{i}{2}|^2 = \tfrac{1}{4} + \tfrac{1}{4} = \tfrac{1}{2} \). The angle difference is 90 degrees, \( \cos 90^\circ = 0 \), so no interference either way.
