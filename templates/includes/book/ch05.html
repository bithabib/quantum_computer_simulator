Chapter 5: Numbers that turn (complex numbers)

Big idea. There is a number \( i \) with \( i^2 = -1 \). Multiplying by \( i \) means "turn a quarter circle". A complex number is an arrow in a flat plane, with a length and an angle. Quantum amplitudes are complex numbers, and the angle is what makes quantum computing work.

A problem with no answer

Solve \( x^2 = -1 \). No ordinary number works: a positive number squared is positive, a negative number squared is also positive, and zero squared is zero. Mathematicians were stuck on this for centuries, until they simply decided to invent a new number and see what happened. They called it \( i \) and gave it one rule:

\[ i^2 = -1 \]

Everything else follows from that rule and normal algebra. For example \( i^3 = i^2 \cdot i = -i \), and \( i^4 = i^2 \cdot i^2 = (-1)(-1) = 1 \). So \( i, i^2, i^3, i^4 \) is \( i, -1, -i, 1 \), and then it repeats. Four steps and you are back where you started. That is a big hint.

A complex number is an arrow

Write a complex number as \( z = a + bi \), with \( a \) and \( b \) ordinary numbers. Call \( a \) the real part and \( b \) the imaginary part. Now draw it: go \( a \) steps right and \( b \) steps up. The number \( 3 + 4i \) is an arrow to the point \( (3, 4) \). Real numbers lie on the horizontal line (\( b = 0 \)). The number \( i \) itself is the arrow straight up to \( (0, 1) \).

Everything you learned about arrows in Chapter 2 applies. The length of \( z \), written \( |z| \), is

\[ |z| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}, \qquad |3 + 4i| = \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5 \]

and adding complex numbers is adding arrows: add the real parts, add the imaginary parts.

\[ (3 + 4i) + (1 - 2i) = 4 + 2i \]

Multiplying means turning

Multiply \( 1 \) by \( i \): you get \( i \). On the picture, the arrow pointing right (to 1) became the arrow pointing up (to \( i \)). It turned a quarter circle anticlockwise. Multiply by \( i \) again: \( i \cdot i = -1 \), the arrow pointing left. Another quarter turn. Again: \( -i \), pointing down. Again: \( 1 \). Four quarter turns make a full circle, which is why the powers of \( i \) repeat every four.

Multiplying by \( i \) turns an arrow by 90 degrees. Multiplying by \( -1 \) turns it by 180 degrees. That is all \( i^2 = -1 \) means: two quarter turns make a half turn.

To multiply two general complex numbers, use ordinary algebra and replace \( i^2 \) by \( -1 \):

\[ (a + bi)(c + di) = ac + adi + bci + bd\,i^2 = (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)i \] \[ (1 + i)(1 + i) = 1 + 2i + i^2 = 1 + 2i - 1 = 2i \]

Look at that last one on the picture. \( 1 + i \) is the arrow at 45 degrees with length \( \sqrt{2} \). Squaring it gave \( 2i \): the arrow at 90 degrees with length 2. The angles added (45 + 45 = 90) and the lengths multiplied (\( \sqrt{2} \times \sqrt{2} = 2 \)). This is always true:

When you multiply complex numbers, lengths multiply and angles add.

Length and angle form

Since a complex number is an arrow, we can describe it by its length \( r \) and its angle \( \theta \) from the horizontal, instead of by \( a \) and \( b \). From the triangle, \( a = r\cos\theta \) and \( b = r\sin\theta \), so

\[ z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta) \]

Leonhard Euler found a shorter way to write the bracket. It turns out that \( \cos\theta + i\sin\theta = e^{i\theta} \), where \( e \approx 2.718 \) is the number from exponential growth. You do not need to know why for this book. Just read \( e^{i\theta} \) as "the unit arrow at angle \( \theta \)". So every complex number is

\[ z = r\,e^{i\theta} \]

and "lengths multiply, angles add" is just the rule for multiplying powers: \( r_1 e^{i\theta_1} \cdot r_2 e^{i\theta_2} = r_1 r_2\, e^{i(\theta_1 + \theta_2)} \). In quantum computing, the angle \( \theta \) is called the phase. Several gates do nothing but change the phase, and you will see in Chapter 12 that phase is where the power hides.

Some unit arrows you will see often:

\[ e^{i \cdot 0} = 1, \qquad e^{i\pi/2} = i, \qquad e^{i\pi} = -1, \qquad e^{i 3\pi/2} = -i \]

(Angles are in radians: \( \pi \) radians is 180 degrees, \( \pi/2 \) is 90 degrees.)

The mirror image: complex conjugate

Flip the sign of the imaginary part and you get the complex conjugate, written \( z^* \) (some books write \( \bar{z} \)):

\[ z = a + bi \quad\Rightarrow\quad z^* = a - bi \]

On the picture, this reflects the arrow in the horizontal line: same length, opposite angle. In length-and-angle form, \( (r e^{i\theta})^* = r e^{-i\theta} \).

Why do we care? Multiply a number by its own conjugate:

\[ z\,z^* = (a + bi)(a - bi) = a^2 - abi + abi - b^2 i^2 = a^2 + b^2 = |z|^2 \]

The imaginary parts cancel and you get the length squared, an ordinary positive number. This is the single most used fact in this book. In the next chapter, the length squared of an amplitude is a probability, and \( z z^* \) is how you compute it.

\[ |z|^2 = z\,z^* \]

Dividing

To divide by a complex number, multiply top and bottom by the conjugate of the bottom. The bottom becomes a real number and you can split it up:

\[ \frac{1}{1 + i} = \frac{1 - i}{(1 + i)(1 - i)} = \frac{1 - i}{1 + 1} = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{2}i \]

You will rarely need this, but it is good to know it is possible.

Complex arrows

Finally, nothing stops the entries of a vector being complex numbers. The arrow \( \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ i \end{pmatrix} \) is perfectly fine. Its length uses \( |z|^2 \) for each entry: \( \sqrt{|1|^2 + |i|^2} = \sqrt{1 + 1} = \sqrt{2} \). Normalised, it is \( \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ i \end{pmatrix} \), a genuine qubit state you will build in the Quantum Lab. Vectors with complex entries live in what physicists call a Hilbert space. The name sounds grand. It just means "arrows with complex entries and a dot product". Chapter 7 makes one small upgrade to the dot product so that it works with complex entries; apart from that, you already know everything about it.

Try it. In the Quantum Lab, open the challenge The +i state. Its goal is the arrow \( \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle + i|1\rangle) \). You will solve it properly in Chapter 10. For now, read the hints and notice that the S gate "adds a 90 degree phase": it multiplies one entry by \( i \).
Check yourself

Q1. What is \( (2 + i)(2 - i) \)?

A1. \( 4 - i^2 = 4 + 1 = 5 \). It is \( |2 + i|^2 \).

Q2. What is \( i \times (3 + 4i) \), and what happened to the arrow?

A2. \( 3i + 4i^2 = -4 + 3i \). The arrow \( (3, 4) \) turned a quarter circle to \( (-4, 3) \). Same length 5.

Q3. What is \( e^{i\pi} + 1 \)?

A3. \( -1 + 1 = 0 \). This is Euler's identity, often called the most beautiful equation in maths.
