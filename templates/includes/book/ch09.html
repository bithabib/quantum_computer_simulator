Chapter 9: Looking at a qubit (measurement)

Big idea. Measuring a qubit gives 0 or 1, at random, with probabilities \( |\alpha|^2 \) and \( |\beta|^2 \). Afterwards the qubit is whatever you saw. You cannot peek without changing it, and you cannot copy it to peek at the copy.

The rule, precisely

Take a qubit \( |\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle \) and measure it in the \( \{|0\rangle, |1\rangle\} \) basis. Two things happen:

  1. You get the result 0 with probability \( |\alpha|^2 \), or 1 with probability \( |\beta|^2 \). Which one is genuinely random; no hidden detail decides it.
  2. The qubit's state becomes the result. If you saw 0, it is now \( |0\rangle \). If you saw 1, it is now \( |1\rangle \). The old \( \alpha \) and \( \beta \) are gone.

Step 2 is called collapse. Measure the same qubit again and you get the same answer with certainty, because it is now a basis state. The randomness happened once.

A worked example

Take \( |\psi\rangle = 0.6|0\rangle + 0.8i\,|1\rangle \). Is it normalised? \( 0.36 + |0.8i|^2 = 0.36 + 0.64 = 1 \). Yes.

  • Probability of 0: \( |0.6|^2 = 0.36 \).
  • Probability of 1: \( |0.8i|^2 = (0.8i)(-0.8i) = 0.64 \).

Measure 100 identical copies and you expect roughly 36 zeros and 64 ones. The \( i \) made no difference to the counts. Phase never shows up in a plain measurement. It only shows up after a gate mixes the two parts, which is what Chapter 10 is for.

Why you must repeat

One measurement tells you almost nothing: a single 1 could come from \( |1\rangle \) or from \( |+\rangle \) or from our 0.6/0.8 state. To learn \( |\beta|^2 \) you prepare the same state many times, measure each copy, and count. A thousand runs pins the probability to about 3 percent. A real quantum computer does exactly this; the simulator on this site reports the exact probabilities instead, which is a luxury hardware does not have.

Measuring in another basis

Nothing forces you to ask "0 or 1?". You can ask "plus or minus?" instead. Chapter 7 gave the general rule: the probability of outcome \( |\phi\rangle \) is \( |\langle\phi|\psi\rangle|^2 \). Measure \( |0\rangle \) in the plus/minus basis:

\[ P(+) = |\langle +|0\rangle|^2 = \left|\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right|^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}, \qquad P(-) = |\langle -|0\rangle|^2 = \tfrac{1}{2} \]

A state that is certain in one basis is a coin flip in another. And measure \( |+\rangle \) in the plus/minus basis: \( P(+) = |\langle +|+\rangle|^2 = 1 \). Certain. So \( |+\rangle \) and \( |-\rangle \), which looked identical when you asked "0 or 1?", are perfectly told apart when you ask "plus or minus?". The phase was there all along; you had to ask the right question.

In practice a quantum computer only measures 0/1. To measure in the plus/minus basis you first apply a gate that turns \( |+\rangle \) into \( |0\rangle \) and \( |-\rangle \) into \( |1\rangle \), then measure 0/1. That gate is \( H \), and you will meet it next chapter.

Measurement as an eigenvalue

Here is where Chapter 3's eigenvectors return. The gate \( Z \) has eigenvectors \( |0\rangle \) and \( |1\rangle \) with eigenvalues \( +1 \) and \( -1 \). A "0/1 measurement" is exactly a "measurement of \( Z \)": the possible results are the eigenvectors, and the number you read is the eigenvalue (\( +1 \) for 0, \( -1 \) for 1). Likewise, a "plus/minus measurement" is a measurement of \( X \), whose eigenvectors are \( |+\rangle \) and \( |-\rangle \). Physicists say "measure \( Z \)" and "measure \( X \)" and mean these two questions. (Chapter 10 says which matrices can be measured this way.)

No peeking, no copying

Could you dodge the collapse by making a copy of the qubit and measuring the copy? No. There is a short proof. It uses linearity from Chapter 3 and one piece of notation from Chapter 11: two qubits side by side are written \( |a\rangle|b\rangle \), meaning "first qubit \( a \), second qubit \( b \)", and brackets of such things multiply out like ordinary algebra.

Suppose a copying machine \( C \) existed. It would have to copy \( |0\rangle \) and \( |1\rangle \): \( C|0\rangle|{\text{blank}}\rangle = |0\rangle|0\rangle \) and \( C|1\rangle|{\text{blank}}\rangle = |1\rangle|1\rangle \). Every gate is a matrix, and matrices are linear, meaning \( C(a + b) = Ca + Cb \). So applying \( C \) to \( |+\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle + |1\rangle) \) must give \( \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle|0\rangle + |1\rangle|1\rangle) \). But a true copy of \( |+\rangle \) would be \( |+\rangle|+\rangle = \tfrac{1}{2}(|0\rangle|0\rangle + |0\rangle|1\rangle + |1\rangle|0\rangle + |1\rangle|1\rangle) \). These are different. So no such \( C \) exists. This is the no-cloning theorem, and it is why quantum information cannot be backed up like a file, and also why someone listening in on a quantum key exchange cannot avoid being noticed.

Try it. In the Quantum Lab, open Equal superposition. Write qc.h(0) and run it. The page shows the measurement probabilities: 50 percent 0, 50 percent 1. That is \( |+\rangle \) measured in the 0/1 basis. Now add a second line, qc.h(0) again, and run. The probabilities return to 100 percent 0. The second H measured "plus or minus?" by turning plus back into zero.
Check yourself

Q1. A qubit \( \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}|0\rangle + \tfrac{1}{2}|1\rangle \) is measured. Probability of 1?

A1. \( \tfrac{1}{4} \). (And \( \tfrac{3}{4} \) for 0; they add to 1.)

Q2. You measured it and got 1. You measure it again. What do you get?

A2. 1, with certainty. The state collapsed to \( |1\rangle \).

Q3. Measure \( |-\rangle \) in the plus/minus basis. Result?

A3. Minus, with certainty: \( |\langle -|-\rangle|^2 = 1 \).
