Chapter 2: Arrows and lists (vectors)

Big idea. A vector is an arrow. You can write an arrow as a short list of numbers. You can add arrows and stretch arrows. Every quantum state in this book is an arrow.

An arrow on a map

Stand in the middle of a football pitch. Walk 3 metres east and 4 metres north. The arrow from where you started to where you ended is a vector. We write it as a column of two numbers:

\[ \vec{v} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix} \]

The top number is how far east, the bottom number is how far north. That is all a vector is: an arrow, written as a list.

How long is the arrow?

Walking 3 east and 4 north makes a right-angled triangle. Pythagoras tells us the straight-line distance:

\[ |\vec{v}| = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5 \]

The vertical bars mean "the length of". For any arrow \( \begin{pmatrix} a \\ b \end{pmatrix} \), the length is \( \sqrt{a^2 + b^2} \). An arrow of length exactly 1 is called a unit vector. Quantum states are always unit vectors. Keep that in mind; it comes back in Chapter 6.

Adding arrows

Walk 3 east and 4 north, then walk 2 east and 1 south. Where are you? 5 east and 3 north. To add arrows, add the numbers in each row:

\[ \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} \]

In a picture, you put the tail of the second arrow on the head of the first, and the sum is the arrow from the very start to the very end.

Stretching arrows

Walk the arrow \( \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix} \) twice. You are now at 6 east, 8 north. Multiplying an arrow by an ordinary number (called a scalar) multiplies every entry:

\[ 2 \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 6 \\ 8 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \tfrac{1}{5} \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.6 \\ 0.8 \end{pmatrix} \]

The second one is useful. Dividing an arrow by its own length gives a unit vector pointing the same way. Check: \( \sqrt{0.6^2 + 0.8^2} = \sqrt{0.36 + 0.64} = 1 \). This is called normalising the arrow. We will do it often.

The two special arrows

Two arrows deserve names: one step east and one step north.

\[ \hat{x} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \hat{y} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \]

Every arrow on the pitch is some amount of \( \hat{x} \) plus some amount of \( \hat{y} \):

\[ \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix} = 3\hat{x} + 4\hat{y} \]

A set of arrows that can build every other arrow this way is called a basis. The numbers in front (3 and 4) are the components. The same arrow can have different components if you pick a different basis, for example if you rotate the map. The arrow itself does not change, only the numbers describing it. This idea is the heart of quantum measurement, so remember it.

The dot product: how much do two arrows agree?

Take two arrows and multiply matching entries, then add:

\[ \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix} \cdot \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} = 3 \times 2 + 4 \times (-1) = 6 - 4 = 2 \]

This number is the dot product. It tells you how much the two arrows point the same way:

  • Big and positive: they point roughly the same way.
  • Zero: they are at right angles. We say they are orthogonal.
  • Negative: they point roughly opposite ways.

Check that \( \hat{x} \cdot \hat{y} = 1 \times 0 + 0 \times 1 = 0 \). East and north are at right angles, as expected. And \( \hat{x} \cdot \hat{x} = 1 \). A basis whose arrows all have length 1 and are all at right angles to each other is called orthonormal. Quantum computing uses orthonormal bases everywhere.

One more fact: the dot product of an arrow with itself is its length squared. \( \vec{v} \cdot \vec{v} = 3^2 + 4^2 = 25 = |\vec{v}|^2 \).

Arrows with more than two numbers

Nothing stops us using three numbers (east, north, up) or ten or a million. The rules are the same: add row by row, stretch every row, dot product is multiply-and-add. We cannot draw a ten-number arrow, but the maths does not care. A quantum computer with 10 qubits uses arrows with 1024 numbers. We will get there.

Meet |0⟩ and |1⟩

Physicists write vectors with a special bracket called a ket: \( |v\rangle \) instead of \( \vec{v} \). It means exactly the same thing. The two basis arrows of a qubit are:

\[ |0\rangle = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad |1\rangle = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \]

They are just \( \hat{x} \) and \( \hat{y} \) with new names. A qubit is an arrow built from them, like \( 0.6|0\rangle + 0.8|1\rangle \). You already know how to add it up:

\[ 0.6 \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + 0.8 \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.6 \\ 0.8 \end{pmatrix} \]

That is a unit vector, so it is a valid qubit. Chapter 8 explains what the 0.6 and 0.8 mean.

Check yourself

Q1. What is the length of \( \begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ 12 \end{pmatrix} \)?

A1. \( \sqrt{25 + 144} = \sqrt{169} = 13 \).

Q2. Normalise it.

A2. \( \begin{pmatrix} 5/13 \\ 12/13 \end{pmatrix} \).

Q3. Is \( \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \) orthogonal to \( \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} \)?

A3. Yes. The dot product is \( 1 - 1 = 0 \). These two arrows will matter a lot in Chapter 10.
