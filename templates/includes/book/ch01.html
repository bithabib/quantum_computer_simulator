Chapter 1: Why do we need new maths?

Big idea. The maths you learn at school describes balls, cars and planets very well. It does not describe atoms. Atoms play by different rules, and to write those rules down we need a few new tools. This book teaches the tools first, then uses them to build a quantum computer on paper.

A ball you can predict

Roll a ball across the floor. If you know how hard you pushed it and how rough the floor is, you can say exactly where it will be one second from now. Push it the same way again and it does the same thing again. This is the world Isaac Newton described, and school physics is mostly about this world.

Figure 1.1: A rolling ball. Same push, same result, every time.

An atom you cannot

About a hundred years ago, scientists started looking inside atoms. Ernest Rutherford fired tiny particles at a thin sheet of gold. Most went straight through, but a few bounced back. He worked out that an atom is mostly empty space with a tiny heavy centre, the nucleus, and electrons somewhere around it.

Figure 1.2: Rutherford's picture. A heavy nucleus with electrons around it.

The obvious guess was that electrons orbit the nucleus like planets orbit the Sun. Niels Bohr improved the picture. He said electrons can only sit on certain fixed "rings", and they jump between rings by taking in or giving out exactly the right amount of light.

Figure 1.3: Bohr's picture. Electrons on fixed rings, jumping between them.

Then the strange part arrived. An electron is not really a tiny ball on a ring. Experiments showed that:

  • You cannot know exactly where an electron is and exactly how fast it moves. Only one or the other.
  • Before you look, an electron can be "spread out" over several places at once.
  • When you look, you find it in one place, but you can only say the chance of each place.
  • Two electrons can be linked so that looking at one instantly tells you about the other, even far apart.

A rolling ball does none of these things. So the old maths of positions and speeds is not enough.

What the new rules look like

Here is the whole of quantum physics in three sentences. Do not worry if they do not make sense yet. The next six chapters exist to make them make sense.

  1. The state of a quantum thing is an arrow (a vector), not a position.
  2. Anything that happens to it is a machine that turns the arrow (a matrix).
  3. When you look, the length of a part of the arrow, squared, is the chance you see that result.

Arrows, machines and chances: that is linear algebra, complex numbers and probability. That is the maths we need, and it is less than you might fear. Most of it is adding and multiplying small lists of numbers.

Why a computer?

An ordinary computer stores a bit: 0 or 1. A quantum computer stores a qubit, which is an arrow that can point partly at 0 and partly at 1 at the same time. With many qubits, the arrow lives in a space with an enormous number of directions, and a quantum computer can turn the whole arrow at once. For some problems, like searching, or breaking codes, or simulating molecules, this is far faster than anything a normal computer can do.

By the end of this book you will have built a qubit, turned it with gates, entangled two of them, and run two real quantum algorithms, all with maths you can check by hand and on the simulator on this site.

How to read this book

  • Part I (Chapters 1 to 7) is the maths. Each chapter is short and builds on the last.
  • Part II (Chapters 8 to 15) is real quantum computing, using only the maths from Part I.
  • Boxes marked Try it send you to the simulators on this site. Use them. Seeing the arrow move is worth a page of words.
  • Boxes marked Check yourself have a question with the answer hidden. Try before you peek.
Check yourself

Q. Name one thing an electron does that a rolling ball never does.

A. Any of: it can be spread over several places before you look; you cannot know both its position and its speed exactly; it can be linked to another electron far away.
