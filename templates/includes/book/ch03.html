Chapter 3: Machines that move arrows (matrices)

Big idea. A matrix is a machine. You feed it an arrow, it gives back a new arrow. Every quantum gate is a matrix. Matrices multiply by a simple "row times column" rule.

A flipping machine

Imagine a machine that swaps east and north. Feed it "3 east, 4 north" and it returns "4 east, 3 north". We can write that machine as a square grid of four numbers:

\[ X = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \]

This grid is a matrix. To use it, we multiply it by the arrow.

The one rule: row times column

To multiply a matrix by a vector, take each row of the matrix, lay it along the vector's column, multiply matching entries, and add. The first row gives the first entry of the answer, the second row gives the second.

\[ \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \times 3 + 1 \times 4 \\ 1 \times 3 + 0 \times 4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} \]

It swapped them. Each row is a dot product with the input arrow, nothing more. In general:

\[ \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} ax + by \\ cx + dy \end{pmatrix} \]

What the columns tell you

Here is a shortcut that makes matrices much less mysterious. Feed the machine the basis arrow \( |0\rangle = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \):

\[ \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} a \\ c \end{pmatrix} \]

You get the first column. Feed it \( |1\rangle \) and you get the second column. So:

The first column of a matrix is where it sends \( |0\rangle \). The second column is where it sends \( |1\rangle \). Because every arrow is a mix of \( |0\rangle \) and \( |1\rangle \), knowing those two answers tells you everything the machine does.

Look at \( X \) again: its first column is \( \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = |1\rangle \) and its second column is \( |0\rangle \). So \( X \) sends 0 to 1 and 1 to 0. It is a NOT gate. In Chapter 10 it will be the first quantum gate you meet.

Some machines worth knowing

The do-nothing machine (identity):

\[ I = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad I \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} \]

The sign-flip machine: keeps \( |0\rangle \), turns \( |1\rangle \) into \( -|1\rangle \).

\[ Z = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad Z \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} x \\ -y \end{pmatrix} \]

The rotation machine: turns every arrow anticlockwise by an angle \( \theta \).

\[ R(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix} \]

Check it with the column rule: the first column is \( (\cos\theta, \sin\theta) \), which is exactly where the east arrow lands after turning by \( \theta \). Rotations keep lengths the same. Machines that keep lengths the same are the only ones allowed in quantum computing, because a quantum state must stay a unit vector. Such matrices are called unitary.

Two machines in a row

Apply \( X \) and then \( Z \) to an arrow. You can do it in two steps:

\[ X \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} y \\ x \end{pmatrix}, \qquad Z \begin{pmatrix} y \\ x \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} y \\ -x \end{pmatrix} \]

Or you can build one combined machine by multiplying the two matrices. The rule is the same as before: each entry of the answer is a row of the left matrix dotted with a column of the right matrix.

\[ ZX = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1\cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 1 & 1 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 0 \\ 0 \cdot 0 + (-1) \cdot 1 & 0 \cdot 1 + (-1) \cdot 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \]

Check: \( \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} y \\ -x \end{pmatrix} \). Same answer, one step.

Order matters. \( ZX \) means "do \( X \) first, then \( Z \)". The matrix nearest the arrow acts first. Try computing \( XZ \) yourself: you get \( \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \), which is different. Putting on socks then shoes is not the same as shoes then socks.

Undoing a machine

\( X \) swaps the two entries. Doing it twice swaps them back. So \( XX = I \): \( X \) is its own undo. The same is true of \( Z \). A machine that undoes \( M \) is written \( M^{-1} \), the inverse. Every quantum gate has an inverse, because a quantum computer must be able to run backwards. For a rotation by \( \theta \), the inverse is a rotation by \( -\theta \).

Flipping a matrix over: the transpose

Swap the rows and columns of a matrix and you get its transpose, written \( M^T \):

\[ \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}^T = \begin{pmatrix} a & c \\ b & d \end{pmatrix} \]

For a rotation, the transpose is the inverse: \( R(\theta)^T = R(-\theta) \). (Check: swapping rows and columns moves the minus sign from the top right to the bottom left, which is what changing the sign of \( \theta \) does.) In Chapter 7 we add one more step, the complex conjugate, and get the "dagger" \( M^\dagger \), which is the inverse of every quantum gate.

Arrows a machine does not turn: eigenvectors

Feed \( Z \) the arrow \( |0\rangle \). It comes back unchanged. Feed it \( |1\rangle \). It comes back as \( -|1\rangle \): same line, flipped direction. For these two arrows, \( Z \) only stretches or flips, it never turns them off their line. Such arrows are the machine's eigenvectors, and the stretch factor (here \( +1 \) and \( -1 \)) is the eigenvalue.

\[ Z|0\rangle = (+1)|0\rangle, \qquad Z|1\rangle = (-1)|1\rangle \]

Now try \( X \). It swaps entries, so \( |0\rangle \) and \( |1\rangle \) are definitely turned. But look at the arrow with equal entries:

\[ X \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad X \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = -\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} \]

So \( X \) has eigenvectors \( (1, 1) \) and \( (1, -1) \) with eigenvalues \( +1 \) and \( -1 \). Normalised, these are the famous "plus" and "minus" states of Chapter 8:

\[ |+\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad |-\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} \]

Why care? When you measure a qubit, the possible answers are exactly the eigenvectors of the thing you measure, and the number you read out is the eigenvalue. Chapter 9 makes this concrete.

How to find eigenvectors (the honest method)

For small matrices you can guess, as we just did. The general method, which you will meet in later maths courses, is: an eigenvector \( v \) with eigenvalue \( \lambda \) satisfies \( Mv = \lambda v \), which can be rearranged to \( (M - \lambda I) v = 0 \). A non-zero \( v \) exists only when the determinant of \( M - \lambda I \) is zero. For a 2 by 2 matrix \( \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} \) the determinant is \( ad - bc \). For \( Z \):

\[ \det \begin{pmatrix} 1-\lambda & 0 \\ 0 & -1-\lambda \end{pmatrix} = (1-\lambda)(-1-\lambda) = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \lambda = 1 \text{ or } \lambda = -1 \]

Then put each \( \lambda \) back in and solve for \( v \). You do not need this method to follow the rest of the book, but it is there if you want it.

Try it. On the Gate Simulator, apply X to the starting qubit and watch the arrow swap from 0 to 1. Apply X again and it swaps back: \( XX = I \). Then try Z on the starting qubit. Nothing visible happens, because \( |0\rangle \) is an eigenvector of Z.
Check yourself

Q1. Compute \( \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} \).

A1. \( \begin{pmatrix} 2 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 2 \\ 0 \cdot 1 + 3 \cdot 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix} \).

Q2. What is \( ZZ \)?

A2. \( I \). Flipping the sign of the second entry twice gives it back.

Q3. Is \( |1\rangle \) an eigenvector of \( X \)?

A3. No. \( X|1\rangle = |0\rangle \), which is a different line, not a stretched \( |1\rangle \).
