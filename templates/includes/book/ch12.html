Chapter 12: Circuits and a first algorithm (Deutsch)

Big idea. A quantum program is a circuit: qubits as wires, gates in time order. Deutsch's algorithm answers a question about a mystery function in one look where a classical computer needs two. The trick is interference: H, the mystery box, H.

Reading a circuit

A circuit diagram has one horizontal wire per qubit, time running left to right, and gates drawn as boxes on the wires. A CNOT is a dot on the control wire joined to a circled plus on the target wire. A meter symbol at the end is a measurement. The Bell pair circuit from Chapter 11 is: 

q0: ─ H ─●─ M
         │
q1: ─────⊕─ M

Read: H on qubit 0, then CNOT with control 0 and target 1, then measure both. The simulator's draw() prints the same thing as a list of lines. Gates on the same wire are applied in order from left to right; in matrix form, that means the leftmost gate's matrix is rightmost in the product (Chapter 3).

The mystery box

Someone gives you a function \( f \) that takes one bit and returns one bit. There are only four possible functions:

Name\( f(0) \)\( f(1) \)Type
always 000constant
always 111constant
copy01balanced
flip10balanced

A sealed box like this is called an oracle. Your question: is \( f \) constant (same answer for both inputs) or balanced (different answers)? You are only allowed to use \( f \) as a sealed box. Classically you must call it twice, \( f(0) \) and \( f(1) \), and compare. There is no way round it: one call tells you one output, and either type is still possible. David Deutsch showed in 1985 that a quantum computer needs only one call.

The box as a gate

A gate must be reversible (unitary), and "replace \( x \) by \( f(x) \)" is not reversible when \( f \) is constant. The standard fix is to use two qubits: an input \( x \) and an answer qubit \( y \), and define the box \( U_f \) by

\[ U_f\,|x\rangle|y\rangle = |x\rangle\,|y \oplus f(x)\rangle \]

where \( \oplus \) is XOR (add, then drop the 2). The input is kept, and \( f(x) \) is XOR-ed into the answer qubit. Applying \( U_f \) twice XORs \( f(x) \) twice, which undoes it, so \( U_f \) is its own inverse. For the four functions, \( U_f \) is: nothing; X on \( y \); CNOT from \( x \) to \( y \); X on \( y \) then CNOT. You can build all four in the Lab.

The trick: phase kickback

Here is the clever step. Put the answer qubit in \( |-\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle - |1\rangle) \) instead of \( |0\rangle \), and send in a basis state \( |x\rangle \):

\[ U_f\,|x\rangle|-\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\big(|x\rangle|0 \oplus f(x)\rangle - |x\rangle|1 \oplus f(x)\rangle\big) \]

If \( f(x) = 0 \), nothing changes: you get \( |x\rangle|-\rangle \). If \( f(x) = 1 \), the two terms swap places, which is the same as a minus sign: \( -|x\rangle|-\rangle \). In one line:

\[ U_f\,|x\rangle|-\rangle = (-1)^{f(x)}\,|x\rangle|-\rangle \]

The answer qubit comes out unchanged, and the answer \( f(x) \) has been moved into the phase of the input qubit. This is called phase kickback. Now the input can be a superposition, and the box will stamp a sign onto each part at once.

Deutsch's algorithm, step by step

q0: |0⟩ ─ H ─┌─────┐─ H ─ M
             │ U_f │
q1: |1⟩ ─ H ─└─────┘─────

Step 0. Start in \( |0\rangle|1\rangle \).

Step 1. H on both. Qubit 0 becomes \( |+\rangle \), qubit 1 becomes \( |-\rangle \):

\[ |+\rangle|-\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\big(|0\rangle + |1\rangle\big)|-\rangle \]

Step 2. Apply the box once. Phase kickback stamps each part:

\[ \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\big((-1)^{f(0)}|0\rangle + (-1)^{f(1)}|1\rangle\big)|-\rangle \]

Now look at the two cases for qubit 0 (ignoring qubit 1, which is just \( |-\rangle \) and no longer matters):

  • Constant (\( f(0) = f(1) \)): both signs equal, so qubit 0 is \( \pm\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle + |1\rangle) = \pm|+\rangle \).
  • Balanced (\( f(0) \neq f(1) \)): signs differ, so qubit 0 is \( \pm\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle - |1\rangle) = \pm|-\rangle \).

The overall \( \pm \) is a global phase and does not matter. So after one call the question "constant or balanced?" has become the question "is qubit 0 plus or minus?".

Step 3. Chapter 9 told us how to ask that: apply H and measure. \( H|+\rangle = |0\rangle \), \( H|-\rangle = |1\rangle \).

Measure qubit 0. Read 0: \( f \) is constant. Read 1: \( f \) is balanced. With certainty, after calling \( f \) once.

Check it with real numbers

Take \( f = \) "copy", so \( U_f \) is a CNOT. Follow the four amplitudes of \( (q_0, q_1) \) in order \( 00, 01, 10, 11 \):

  • Start: \( |01\rangle = (0, 1, 0, 0) \).
  • After H on both: \( |+\rangle|-\rangle = \tfrac{1}{2}(1, -1, 1, -1) \).
  • After CNOT (swap the last two entries, since control 1 flips the target): \( \tfrac{1}{2}(1, -1, -1, 1) \). That is \( |-\rangle|-\rangle \).
  • After H on qubit 0: \( H|-\rangle = |1\rangle \), so the state is \( |1\rangle|-\rangle \). Qubit 0 reads 1: balanced. Correct.

Now take \( f = \) "always 0", so \( U_f \) does nothing. After the H gates the state is \( |+\rangle|-\rangle \), unchanged by the box, and the final H sends qubit 0 to \( |0\rangle \). Reads 0: constant. Correct.

Why it works, in one sentence

The first H lets you ask about both inputs at once; the box writes the answers into the phases; the second H turns a phase difference into a bit by interference. Every algorithm in this book has this shape: spread out, stamp phases, interfere, read.

From one bit to many: Deutsch-Jozsa

Make the function take \( n \) bits, and promise it is either constant or balanced (1 on exactly half the inputs). Classically, in the worst case you must check \( 2^{n-1} + 1 \) inputs. The quantum circuit is the same picture with \( n \) input wires: H on all, one call of the box, H on all, measure. Constant gives all zeros; balanced gives anything else. One call, for any \( n \). The derivation is the same as above with \( n \) signs instead of two; the key fact is that after the final H gates, the amplitude of \( |00\ldots0\rangle \) is the average of all the \( (-1)^{f(x)} \), which is \( \pm 1 \) for constant and exactly 0 for balanced.

Try it. In the Quantum Lab, build Deutsch's algorithm on two qubits for the "copy" function: 
qc = QuantumCircuit(2)
qc.x(1)        # answer qubit to |1>
qc.h(0)
qc.h(1)
qc.cx(0, 1)    # the box: f(x) = x
qc.h(0)
The probabilities should show qubit 0 (the left digit) is 1 with certainty. Now delete the qc.cx(0, 1) line (the "always 0" box) and run again: qubit 0 is 0 with certainty. Then try the "flip" box: qc.x(0); qc.cx(0, 1); qc.x(0).
Check yourself

Q1. Why must the answer qubit start in \( |-\rangle \) and not \( |0\rangle \)?

A1. With \( |0\rangle \) the box writes \( f(x) \) into the answer qubit as a bit, and the two parts of the input become entangled with different answers, so no phase appears on qubit 0 and the final H cannot interfere them. \( |-\rangle \) turns the answer into a sign instead.

Q2. What does Deutsch's algorithm output for \( f = \) "always 1"?

A2. 0, because it is constant. The box multiplies both parts by \( -1 \), a global phase.

Q3. Could you learn \( f(0) \) itself from this algorithm?

A3. No. It tells you whether \( f(0) = f(1) \), nothing more. Quantum computers answer some questions faster, not every question.
