\section{Why not a single-parameter label}
A simpler label, used
in an earlier preprint of this work, is the variance of a single parameter, the rotation on qubit
$0$ in layer $\lfloor L/2\rfloor$, estimated by parameter shift. We keep that
quantity as a secondary column of the dataset. In \LegacyFloorFrac\% of specs it is a
structural zero, and a label built on it has to clip every such case to
a floor, where it is indistinguishable from an extremely flat landscape. Figure~S3 compares the two
labels. Where the single-parameter gradient is not a structural zero, the two
labels agree closely (correlation \LegacyCorr, mean absolute difference
\LegacyMAD). Where it is a structural zero, \LegacyFloorTrainable\% of the
circuits are in fact trainable by the all-parameter label. A
single-parameter label therefore mixes two different phenomena, and the
all-parameter label removes the mixture.

\section{Simulator and label validation}
Statevectors are evolved exactly with a small in-house NumPy simulator
(\texttt{qsim} in the released code), which applies each gate as a tensor
contraction on the $2^n$-amplitude state. We validate it in four ways,
reproducible with two commands in the repository. (i)~On \ValN{} random
circuits with up to seven qubits, the statevector agrees with an independent
dense-matrix simulation that builds the full $2^n\times2^n$ unitary of every
gate, to a maximum amplitude difference of \ValState. (ii)~On \ValN{} random
circuits, the adjoint gradient agrees with the parameter-shift rule of
the parameter-shift formula for every one of \ValGradN{} parameters, to a maximum
difference of \ValGrad. (iii)~Every parameter flagged as a structural zero
from 50 samples remained below $10^{-10}$ on 200 fresh samples, in all
\ValStructN{} flagged cases. (iv)~The Clifford estimator of
main-text Section~\ref{M-sec:cliffordmethod}, with \ClValSamples{} grid samples,
reproduces the label from \ClValSvSamples{} exact adjoint gradients on
\ClValN{} random circuits to a mean absolute difference of \ClValMAD{} in
$\log_{10}$ units (maximum \ClValMax; \ClValBelow{} of the circuits has a
variance below the Clifford resolution at this sample size and is not
included in the comparison), and none of the \ClValStruct{}
parameters that the exact gradients flagged as structural zeros produced a
single non-zero Clifford sample.

\section{Extrapolation with a widening gap}
Figure~S8 (Table~S5) repeats the experiment with the training cutoff moved down: train
on $n\le k$ and test on every larger circuit, for $k=6,\dots,10$. HGB falls
from \GapHGBkTen{} at $k=10$ to \GapHGBkSix{} at $k=6$, no better than the
training mean. The structured linear model is the most stable, from
\GapStructkTen{} to \GapStructkSix. The MLP is accurate for small gaps
($\GapMLPmeankTen\pm\GapMLPsdkTen$ at $k=10$) but becomes unreliable for
large ones: at $k=6$, where 7- to 12-qubit circuits are predicted from 2-
to 6-qubit ones, ten seeds give $\GapMLPmeankSix\pm\GapMLPsdkSix$ with a
range from \GapMLPminkSix{} to \GapMLPmaxkSix. A neural network can
extrapolate, but how well depends on its initialization, and a user cannot
know in advance which seed is the good one. For prediction well beyond the
training range, the model with an explicit scaling form is the safer
choice.

\section{Learning curve}
Figure~S1 shows grouped-CV $R^2$ as a function of the
number of training rows, with whole architectures added at a time. Both
models reach $R^2>0.9$ with about \LCRowsNinety{} rows and are within
$0.02$ of their final value by \LCRowsSaturate{} rows; the MLP is the more
data-efficient of the two below 2\,000 rows (\LCMLPFiveHundred{} versus
\LCHGBFiveHundred{} at 500 rows). For a user extending the predictor to a
new circuit family, this sets the expectation: a few thousand labeled
circuits, which the released generator produces in well under an hour on a
laptop for $n\le12$, suffice to reach the plateau.
