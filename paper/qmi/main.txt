                                    Abstract
Barren plateaus, the exponential decay of the cost-gradient variance with qubit
number, are a central obstacle to training variational quantum circuits. Diag-
nosing them by sampling gradients is costly when many candidate circuits must
be screened. We ask how well the gradient variance of a hardware-eﬀicient cir-
cuit can be predicted from its architecture alone: qubit count, depth, rotation
scheme, entanglement pattern, entangler gate, and cost observable. We simu-
late 20 000 random circuits with 2 to 12 qubits, compute the exact gradient
of every parameter, and use the mean gradient variance over all parameters
as the label, excluding parameters whose gradient vanishes identically. Under
architecture-grouped cross-validation, gradient-boosted trees and a neural net-
work reach R2 = 0.980 against a noise ceiling of 0.985. A structured linear
model reaches 0.969, so the map from architecture to gradient variance is sim-
ple, whereas a five-coeﬀicient rule in qubit count, depth, and cost locality reaches
0.682. Part of the difference is that the last entangling layer changes the weight
of the measured observable. Trained on at most 12 qubits, models with a contin-
uous dependence on qubit count reach R2 ≈ 0.972 on held-out 13- and 14-qubit
circuits; tree ensembles do not extrapolate. With exact Clifford-sampled labels
for circuits up to 32 qubits, models trained on at most 12 qubits remain accu-
rate for about eight more qubits and fail beyond 24; trained on up to 20 qubits
they predict 21- to 32-qubit circuits with R2 ≈ 0.9. Graph descriptors allow
partial transfer to entanglement patterns absent from training. At fixed qubit
count the predictor ranks circuits with Spearman correlation 0.94 to 0.98. The
predicted quantity is a finite-size gradient-variance proxy for one circuit family,
not optimization success and not a proof of scaling.

1 Introduction
Variational quantum algorithms (VQAs) are the leading way to use noisy
intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) hardware [1]. A VQA works in three steps.
It encodes a problem into a parameterized quantum circuit, called an ansatz. It
then measures a cost function C(θ) = ⟨ψ(θ)|O|ψ(θ)⟩. Finally, a classical optimizer
updates the parameters θ. This loop underlies variational quantum eigensolvers,
quantum approximate optimization, and most quantum machine-learning (QML)
classifiers [2–4].
     One problem stops this loop from scaling: the barren plateau (BP) [5, 6]. On
a barren plateau the variance of the cost gradient, taken over random parameter
settings, vanishes exponentially in the number of qubits. The loss landscape becomes
almost flat almost everywhere, and gradient-based training stalls. Several causes are
known. Global cost observables trigger barren plateaus [7]. So do highly expressive or
deep ansätze [8], poor initialization [9], and hardware noise [10]. Recent theory unifies
many of these results through the dynamical Lie algebra of the circuit [11, 12]. The
common thread is that a barren plateau is largely a property of the circuit design
rather than of one particular parameter setting.
     The usual way to check a circuit is to measure it. One samples many random
parameter vectors, evaluates the gradients, and estimates their variance. This is
exactly the cost one wants to avoid when screening a large design space, and it is hard-
est for many qubits, where barren plateaus matter most. This motivates our question.
Can a classical model predict the empirical gradient variance of a variational circuit
from its architecture alone, without sampling any gradients?
     We answer this question empirically for one widely used circuit family, the
hardware-eﬀicient ansatz [13]. We treat trainability prediction as a supervised learning
problem over circuit specifications. The features are cheap architectural descriptors:
small integers and categories. The label is the gradient variance, computed once,
offline, by exact statevector simulation. Throughout, we use two standard machine-
learning scores. The coeﬀicient of determination R2 is the fraction of label variance
a regressor explains; R2 = 1 is perfect and R2 = 0 is no better than predicting the
mean. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) is the prob-
ability that a classifier ranks a randomly chosen positive example above a randomly
chosen negative one; 0.5 is chance and 1 is perfect. We also report the mean absolute
error (MAE) of the regressor, in log10 units.
     Two methodological points shape the study. The first is the label. The variance of
the gradient with respect to a single parameter can vanish identically for structural
reasons, for example when the rotation commutes with everything between it and
the observable, and such a structural zero is not a barren plateau. We therefore use
the mean gradient variance over all parameters, with structural zeros detected and
excluded. The second is the evaluation. The sampled design space contains far fewer

distinct architectures than the dataset has rows, so a row-wise random split places
identical feature vectors on both sides. All headline numbers come from architecture-
grouped cross-validation, and every learner is compared with controls of increasing
strength, from the training mean to a structured linear model.
    A word on terminology. We use trainability in the operational sense common in the
barren-plateau literature: the magnitude of the gradient variance. We do not claim to
predict whether a particular optimizer, with a particular budget, succeeds.
    The contributions of this article are as follows.
• We formulate the prediction of gradient variance from architecture as a supervised
  task over one hardware-eﬀicient circuit family. The target is the log10 mean gradient
  variance over all parameters (regression), or a binary flag obtained by thresholding
  it (classification).
• We release a reproducible pipeline that generates a labeled dataset of 20 000 random
  circuits with an exact statevector simulator and adjoint differentiation, and that
  separates structural zeros from small non-zero gradients.
• Under architecture-grouped cross-validation, gradient-boosted trees and a neural
  network reach R2 = 0.980 against a noise ceiling of 0.985. We show that this
  accuracy does not require a flexible learner: a structured linear model with one set
  of coeﬀicients per categorical cell reaches R2 = 0.969. The map from architecture
  to gradient variance is simple, and we say what makes it so.
• We identify one reason the simplest physical rule falls short. The last entangling
  layer is Clifford and changes the weight of the measured observable, so a nominally
  local cost can be effectively global and vice versa. The effective weight is a closed-
  form feature.
• We test extrapolation in qubit count, on statevector-labeled circuits up to 14 qubits
  and, using exact Clifford sampling, on circuits up to 32 qubits, and we report where
  each model class fails.
• We test transfer to entanglement patterns absent from training, using closed-form
  descriptors of the entangling graph and a leave-one-pattern-out experiment over
  five patterns.
    We also want to be clear about scope. We make no claim of quantum advantage.
The model is classical and the data is classically simulated. The predicted quan-
tity is an empirical, finite-size proxy for trainability within one circuit family, not a
proof of exponential scaling. The contribution is a data-driven diagnostic for a known
quantum-computing bottleneck [14, 15].

2.2 Barren plateaus
A circuit family has a barren plateau if Varθ [∂C/∂θk ] decays exponentially in n [5].
This is a statement about a family of circuits of growing size, not about any single
circuit. Cerezo et al. [7] proved that global cost functions cause barren plateaus even
at shallow depth, whereas local costs can stay trainable up to O(log n) depth. Cost
locality is therefore a first-order design knob. Expressibility [8] and entanglement also
correlate with flatter landscapes. Some structured architectures avoid the problem;
quantum convolutional networks are a proven example [16]. The dimension of the
dynamical Lie algebra generated by the circuit gives a unified, exact characterization
for many families [11, 12], and a recent review consolidates these results [6].
    Most of this work is analytical. It derives variance scaling laws for one ansatz
family at a time. Our work is empirical and complementary. We do not prove a
bound. Instead, we learn a predictive map across a heterogeneous but finite space of
architectures, at circuit sizes where the ground truth can be simulated exactly.

2.3 Exact gradients
For gates generated by an operator with two eigenvalues, the parameter-shift rule
gives the exact gradient from two circuit evaluations [17, 18]:

                         ∂C      [                             ]
                             = 12 C(θ + π2 ek ) − C(θ − π2 ek ) .                    (2)
                         ∂θk

Evaluating Eq. (2) for all nL parameters costs 2nL simulations per parameter vector.
On a classical simulator the same exact gradient vector is available from a single
backward pass. Reverse-mode (adjoint) differentiation walks the circuit backwards
once, maintaining the state and the adjoint state, and yields every ∂C/∂θk for roughly
the cost of three forward simulations. We use adjoint differentiation for the dataset
and verify it against Eq. (2) (Section 3.3). Each individual gradient is therefore exact
to floating-point precision. The label, a variance over finitely many random parameter
vectors, is nonetheless an estimator with its own sampling uncertainty, which we
quantify in Section 3.2.

2.4 Related data-driven approaches
Several lines of work use classical computation to rank or choose circuits before train-
ing. Quantum architecture search (QAS) optimizes the circuit structure itself, by
differentiable relaxation [19], by sampling from a supernet [20], or with training-free
proxies that score candidate circuits without optimizing them [21]. Expressibility and
entangling capability are classical descriptors that are estimated by sampling and
used to compare ansätze [22]. Lie-algebraic theory provides an exact, closed-form
trainability criterion for circuits whose generators are known [11, 12].
    Our work differs in three ways. The target is the empirical gradient variance itself,
not downstream task accuracy and not a proxy for it. The input is a handful of
architectural integers and categories, not a sampled statistic such as expressibility, so
a prediction costs microseconds. And the predictor is learned across a heterogeneous
design space and tested on architectures it has never seen, including larger qubit
counts. We are not aware of prior work that casts barren-plateau severity as a directly
predicted regression or classification target from cheap architectural features and that
also tests extrapolation across qubit count. We regard the QAS proxies and the Lie-
algebraic criteria as complementary: a learned predictor of the kind studied here could
serve as a zero-cost filter inside a QAS loop.

Eﬀicient computation of gradient variance.
For some circuit families the gradient variance can be bounded or computed without
simulating a 2n -dimensional state. Uvarov and Biamonte [23] bound it through the
causal cone of each Pauli term of the cost. Napp [24] relates the landscape flatness
of unstructured ansätze to a random walk and estimates it by Monte Carlo. Letcher
et al. [25] derive tight and eﬀiciently computable gradient bounds for a large class of
circuits without design assumptions. The circuits studied here admit a particularly
simple exact method. Every angle appears once with a Pauli generator, so the cost is a
trigonometric polynomial of degree one in each angle, the squared gradient has degree
two, and its uniform average equals its average over the four angles {0, π/2, π, 3π/2}.
At those angles every gate is Clifford and the observable can be propagated as a
single Pauli string at any n. We use this in Section 4.3 to label circuits far beyond
statevector reach. It also bears on the motivation for a learned predictor, which we
discuss in Section 5.

2.5 Trainability, simulability, and honest framing
Recent work urges caution about advantage claims. Cerezo et al. [26] argue that
the structure which removes barren plateaus often also makes a model classically
simulable. Bowles et al. [15] show that out-of-the-box classical models often match or
beat QML models on standard tasks. Schuld and Killoran [14] argue that the field
should de-emphasize “beating classical”. We adopt this stance. The object we predict
is a property of quantum circuits; the predictor and its value are entirely classical.

3 Methods
Figure 1 summarizes the pipeline from a sampled circuit specification to a predicted
trainability value.

3.1 Circuit specification and feature space
Each datapoint is a specification, or spec. A spec fixes one circuit family through
six design parameters: the qubit count n, the layer count L, the rotation scheme,
the entanglement pattern, the entangler gate, and the cost observable. Table 1 lists
the twelve-element feature vector we hand to the model. Six entries are the design
parameters themselves, which we call the primitive features. Six are derived from them
in closed form; two of these, the effective observable weight and the causal cone, encode
physics that the primitive features carry only implicitly. No quantum simulation is
needed to obtain any feature.




                                                   6
Rotation scheme.
With the fixed-ry scheme every rotation in Eq. (1) is Ry . With the random-Pauli
scheme the axis aℓ,q of each of the nL rotations is drawn independently and uniformly
from {x, y, z} once, when the spec is created, from the spec’s own random seed.
The axis pattern is then fixed for that spec: it does not change between the random
parameter vectors used to label the circuit. Two random-Pauli specs with the same
feature vector can therefore have different axis patterns. The feature vector does not
encode the pattern, so this variation acts as label noise from the model’s point of
view. We quantify its effect in Section 3.4.

Entanglement pattern.
Each layer applies the entangler gate to a fixed list of ordered qubit pairs (a, b), in
list order, after the rotation layer. The linear pattern uses the n − 1 nearest-neighbor
pairs (0, 1), (1, 2), . . . , (n − 2, n − 1). The circular pattern adds the wrap-around pair
(n − 1, 0), giving n gates per layer. The all-to-all pattern uses every pair (a, b) with
a < b, giving n(n − 1)/2 gates per layer. For CX the first qubit of each pair is the
control and the second is the target; CZ is symmetric. The same list is used in every
layer.

Cost observable and its effective weight.
The local cost measures Z on qubit 0 only. The global cost measures the parity opera-
tor Z ⊗n . These are the nominal observables. The last entangling layer sits between the
final rotations and the measurement, and it is a Clifford circuit, so it can be absorbed
                                                       †
into the observable: C = ⟨ψ ′ |Oeﬀ |ψ ′ ⟩ with Oeﬀ = Uent O Uent and |ψ ′ ⟩ the state after
the final rotations. CZ is diagonal and leaves a Z-string unchanged. CX maps Z on its
target to Z on control and target, so a layer of CX gates maps the nominal Z-string
to a different Z-string. We call the number of qubits on which Oeﬀ acts the effective
weight. It follows from the design parameters in closed form and can differ sharply
from the nominal weight: with circular CX entanglement the nominally local cost has
effective weight n − 1, and with all-to-all CX entanglement the nominally global cost
has effective weight 1 (Table 7).

Causal cone.
A rotation can only affect the cost if some sequence of entangling gates connects it
to the support of the observable. Propagating the observable backward through the
circuit, and keeping for every qubit the set of Pauli types it can carry (a per-qubit
relaxation that drops correlations between qubits and therefore gives an outer bound),
yields two closed-form quantities: the number of qubits that can carry a non-identity
Pauli when the observable reaches the input, which we call cone_qubits, and the
fraction of rotations at which the incoming Pauli can anticommute with the rotation
generator, cone_frac. For the fixed-ry scheme the rotation axes are known and the
bound is tight: cone_frac equals the exact non-structural fraction of parameters in
90% of circuits, with mean absolute difference 0.022. For random-Pauli circuits the
axes are hidden and the generic case is assumed (mean absolute difference 0.159). The
causal cone matters for extrapolation: for a local observable and a shallow circuit the


                                             7
cone is smaller than the circuit, and once it is, adding qubits adds only parameters
with zero gradient. Uvarov and Biamonte [23] bound the gradient variance through
the same quantity.

3.2 Label generation
We label each spec in four steps. First, we draw 200 parameter vectors θ ∼
Unif[0, 2π)nL . Second, for each θ we compute the exact gradient vector ∇θ C by adjoint
differentiation. Third, for every parameter k we compute the population variance vk
of ∂C/∂θk over the 200 samples. Fourth, we detect structural zeros and average the
remaining variances.

Structural zeros.
Some rotations have a gradient that is identically zero for every θ. This happens when
the rotation commutes with everything between it and the observable: for example
an Rz applied immediately before a Z measurement on the same qubit, a rotation on
a qubit outside the light cone of a local observable in a shallow circuit, or a rotation
on the control qubit of CX gates that lead only to the observable’s support. Such a
parameter is untrainable, but the reason is an exact symmetry, not a barren plateau.
We flag parameter k as a structural zero when maxθ |∂C/∂θk | < 10−10 over all 200
samples. Section 3.3 verifies that flagged parameters remain exactly zero on fresh
samples, so the flag is a property of the circuit and not of the sample. At n ≤ 12 no
genuine barren-plateau variance in our data comes within eight orders of magnitude
of this tolerance, so the two populations do not overlap.

data the mean label crosses this value between six and seven qubits. The regression
target is threshold-free, and Supplementary Table S3 reports the effect of moving the
cutoff.

Sampling uncertainty of the label.
Each vk is an estimate from 200 samples. To measure the resulting label noise directly,
we relabeled 200 randomly chosen specs with an independent set of 200 parameter
vectors. The mean absolute difference between the two labels was 0.021 in log10 units,
and the largest difference was 0.20. This is small compared with the label’s spread

across architectures (standard deviation 0.94) and with the model errors reported
below.

Comparison with a single-parameter label.
A simpler label, used in an earlier preprint of this work, is the variance of a single
parameter, the rotation on qubit 0 in layer ⌊L/2⌋, estimated by parameter shift. We
keep that quantity as a secondary column of the dataset. In 9.6% of specs it is a
structural zero, and a label built on it has to clip every such case to a floor, where
it is indistinguishable from an extremely flat landscape. Supplementary Figure S3
compares the two labels. Where the single-parameter gradient is not a structural zero,
the two labels agree closely (correlation 0.963, mean absolute difference 0.16). Where
it is a structural zero, 69% of the circuits are in fact trainable by the all-parameter
label. A single-parameter label therefore mixes two different phenomena, and the
all-parameter label removes the mixture.

3.3 Simulator and validation
Statevectors are evolved exactly with a small in-house NumPy simulator (qsim in the
released code), which applies each gate as a tensor contraction on the 2n -amplitude
state. We validate it in three ways, reproducible with one command in the repository.
(i) On 200 random circuits with up to seven qubits, the statevector agrees with an
independent dense-matrix simulation that builds the full 2n × 2n unitary of every
gate, to a maximum amplitude difference of 3.1e-16. (ii) On 200 random circuits,
the adjoint gradient agrees with the parameter-shift rule of Eq. (2) for every one of
4 792 parameters, to a maximum difference of 1.0e-15. (iii) Every parameter flagged
as a structural zero from 50 samples remained below 10−10 on 200 fresh samples,
in all 1153 flagged cases. (iv) The Clifford estimator of Section 3.6, with 65 536 grid
samples, reproduces the label from 400 exact adjoint gradients on 200 random circuits
to a mean absolute difference of 0.009 in log10 units (maximum 0.12), and none of
the 2 804 parameters that the exact gradients flagged as structural zeros produced a
single non-zero Clifford sample.

3.4 Dataset and data description
We sample 20 000 specs. Each design parameter is drawn independently and uniformly
over the ranges in Table 2: n uniformly on {2, . . . , 12}, L uniformly on {1, . . . , 20},
and each categorical choice uniformly over its options. Every spec is labeled with 200
gradient samples. Generation is embarrassingly parallel; every spec is seeded repro-
ducibly from a root seed, and completed rows are appended to a comma-separated
text file (CSV) as they finish, so a long run can be resumed.

Architectures versus rows.
The design space has only 5 280 distinct feature vectors, and the 20 000 rows cover
5 168 of them, with a median of 4 and a maximum of 13 rows per architecture. Rows
sharing an architecture differ only in their random-Pauli axis pattern (if applicable)

separately for local and global costs. The physics-informed linear model fits

                                                11
Architecture-grouped cross-validation.
Our primary protocol is K-fold cross-validation with K = 5 in which the folds are
formed from whole architectures. Every row is assigned to one fold according to its
feature vector, so no architecture appears in both the training and the test fold. Each
model is trained on K − 1 folds and scored on the remaining fold, and every row is
scored exactly once. We repeat the whole procedure 2 times with a different random
assignment of architectures to folds, giving 10 scores per model, and report their mean
and standard deviation. The standard deviation measures split-to-split variability.

Row-wise random split (for contrast only).
We also report a single random split of rows with 20% held out. As explained above,
this split places identical feature vectors on both sides, and its scores are upper bounds
on generalization.

Extrapolation across qubit count.
We train on all circuits with n ≤ 10 and test on all circuits with n ∈ {11, 12}. This
split is defined purely by qubit count, so no large-circuit information enters training.
We report scores for the whole test set and separately for n = 11, n = 12, local cost,
and global cost, with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals from 1000 resamples of the
test rows, and the spread over random seeds of the models (ten seeds for the neural
network, whose out-of-range predictions depend on its initialization). The controls are
fitted on the same training set; the linear controls extrapolate in n by construction,
so they are the natural yardstick here.

Feature ablation.
To ask which architectural properties carry the signal, we retrain HGB under the
grouped protocol with a group of features removed and report the drop in R2 . This
avoids the known failure of permutation importance when features are strongly depen-
dent, as ours are: nL, L/n, and the entangler count are deterministic functions of the
primitive features.

Transfer to unseen entanglement patterns.
A categorical pattern index cannot be evaluated on a pattern the model has never
seen, because the index carries no information about what the new pattern is. To
ask whether the predictor transfers beyond the three sampled patterns, we replace
the index by four closed-form descriptors of the entangling graph, which are defined
for any pattern: the mean number of entangling gates per layer, the edge density of
the union graph over layers (edges divided by n(n − 1)/2), its maximum degree, and
its diameter (n if disconnected). We then generate 9 926 additional circuits with two
further patterns that are structurally different from the first three. Brickwork applies
the even nearest-neighbor pairs (0, 1), (2, 3), . . . on even-numbered layers and the odd
pairs (1, 2), (3, 4), . . . on odd-numbered layers, so each layer has about n/2 gates and
the union graph is the chain. Star applies (0, q) for q = 1, . . . , n − 1 in every layer, so
qubit 0 has degree n−1 and the diameter is 2. Everything else is sampled as in Table 2.

3.6 Exact labels beyond statevector reach
Statevector simulation limits the labeled circuits to n ≤ 14. For this circuit family a
second exact method has no such limit. Every angle appears once and every rotation
generator is a Pauli, so C(θ) is a trigonometric polynomial of degree one in each angle
and (∂C/∂θk )2 has degree at most two. The four-point rule θj ∈ {0, π/2, π, 3π/2}
integrates such polynomials exactly, so

where we used that the mean gradient vanishes and that on the grid every gate is
Clifford, so the observable propagates as a single Pauli string whose expectation in
|0⟩⊗n is 0 or ±1 and every gradient component is 0 or ±1. We estimate the right-hand
side by Monte Carlo over the grid, tracking only Pauli types (two bits per qubit),
packing 64 samples per machine word and handling all nL parameters in one backward
sweep; the cost is independent of 2n , and the heaviest 32-qubit circuit takes a fraction
of a second per few thousand samples. Section 3.3 reports the agreement with exact
gradients.
    What the estimator cannot do is resolve a variance far below the reciprocal of
the number of samples times parameters. Sampling is therefore adaptive: each circuit
is sampled until 2 000 non-zero gradient events have been seen or 220 samples have
been drawn, and a circuit with fewer than 100 events at the cap is censored: only an
upper bound on its label is known. Because structural zeros cannot be certified by
sampling at large n, the label in this experiment is the mean gradient variance over
all parameters, log10 Varall , which the estimator measures without bias. It differs from
the main label by log10 of the non-structural fraction, which the causal-cone feature
approximates in closed form. We generated 7 941 circuits with n ∈ [2, 12] for training
and 6 000 with n ∈ [13, 32] for testing, sampled as in Table 2 otherwise.

Local costs: a genuine horizon.
For local costs the structured linear model reaches R2 = 0.92 on 13–16 qubits and
0.89 on 17–20 qubits, eight qubits beyond training, then 0.78 at 21–24, 0.43 at 25–28
and 0.23 at 29–32, with a bias that grows steadily from −0.14 to −0.77. The neural
network matches it up to 20 qubits and then becomes seed-dependent and worse. The
cause is visible in the shallowest circuits. For local costs with L ≤ 3 the true mean
label is flat, −2.07 at 13–16 qubits and −2.03 at 29–32, because the causal cone stops
growing and additional qubits change nothing that the observable can see. The model
predicts −2.13 and −2.52: it continues, more weakly, the decay in qubit count that it
learned below twelve qubits. The cone features reduce this error but do not remove
it, since below twelve qubits the cone is rarely much smaller than the circuit and the
model has few examples of saturation to learn from.

Global costs: accurate where measurable.
For global costs the structured model reaches R2 = 0.87 and 0.84 at 13–16 and 17–
20 qubits, with negligible bias. Beyond that most circuits are censored and the only
available test is whether a prediction respects the measured upper bound on the label.
At 21–24, 25–28 and 29–32 qubits, 35%, 60% and 98% of the predictions for censored
circuits do. Just beyond the resolution limit the model therefore predicts a variance
that is too large, and at the largest sizes it is consistent with the bound. Tree ensembles
predict a constant outside their training range; their apparent scores on the resolved
circuits at large n (Supplementary Table S7) reflect the same survivorship and should
not be read as extrapolation.

simulate exactly is reliable for roughly eight more qubits, which is the regime where
labeling by statevector simulation becomes impractical, and is unreliable beyond that.
The remedy is cheap for this family: when the training range is extended to n ≤ 20
with Clifford labels, the same models predict 21- to 32-qubit circuits with R2 = 0.90
(structured linear), 0.85 ± 0.07 (neural network, five seeds) and 0.94 (HGB), because

4.4 What drives trainability
Table 8 and Figure 5 report the feature ablation, in two parts. The first part asks
what the derived features add to the six design parameters. In-family they add almost
nothing: the six primitive features alone reach R2 = 0.980, and adding the effective
weight, the causal cone, or all derived features changes this by 0.002 or less. A tree
ensemble reconstructs these quantities from the primitives when it has seen every
architecture’s neighbors. Their value appears out of range: they raise transfer to unseen
entanglement patterns (Section 4.5) and extrapolation in qubit count (Sections 4.2
and 4.3), where the model must rely on structure it cannot interpolate.
    The second part removes one primitive feature at a time from the six-feature model.
Two cautions apply to its reading. The drop measures information that the remaining
features cannot supply, so a feature that is implied by the others scores low however
relevant it is. And a large drop does not mean that the variable explains the label on
its own; the cost-locality subgroup mean of Table 4 scores only R2 = 0.043. Most of

4.7 Ranking circuits at fixed qubit count
The intended use of the predictor is to rank candidate architectures and pass the
most promising ones to a more expensive evaluation. A global ranking is not an
informative test of this use. Every circuit in the dataset with n ≤ 5 lies above the
cutoff, so ranking by qubit count alone selects a top fifth that is entirely above the
cutoff (100% precision, 35% recall), and the predictor does the same. The informative
question is whether the predictor ranks circuits correctly within a fixed qubit count,
where size carries no information. Table 10 answers it with the out-of-fold predictions
of Section 4.1. For every n at which both classes occur, the rank correlation between
the HGB prediction and the true label lies between 0.94 and 0.98, against 0.58 to
0.64 for the five-coeﬀicient model; the structured linear model is again close to HGB
(0.91 to 0.97). At n = 12, where only 26% of circuits are above the cutoff, 99.5% of
the top fifth of the HGB ranking are. The top fifth is a shortlist, not a complete list:
by construction it contains at most a fifth of the circuits and so misses the remaining
ones above the cutoff.

    This suggests a practical workflow. First, screen a large space of candidate ansätze
with the learned model, at microseconds per candidate. Then reserve expensive
gradient-variance estimation for the shortlist it flags as trainable. Section 4.7 shows
that the ranking is reliable at fixed qubit count. Within a QAS loop, the predictor
could act as a zero-cost filter ahead of any training-free proxy or actual training.
    Several limitations scope these claims. (i) One circuit family. The design space is
one hardware-eﬀicient ansatz with five fixed entanglement patterns. Section 4.5 shows
that a pattern the model has never seen can be predicted when it is described by
its effect on the observable and on the causal cone, but with a measurable loss, so
predictions for a new gate pattern should be supported by some labeled circuits of
that pattern. The pipeline generates them from one command. Predictions outside the
hardware-eﬀicient family (for example, problem-inspired or Hamiltonian-variational
ansätze) are unsupported. (ii) Finite size. Statevector labels exist only at n ≤ 14.
Clifford labels reach any n but resolve only variances above 100/(220 nL), between
about 10−7 and 10−5 depending on the number of parameters. A barren plateau
is a statement about scaling as n → ∞; our label is a finite-size proxy, and the
extrapolation results cover two additional qubits with statevector labels and about
eight with Clifford labels, not an asymptotic regime. Tree ensembles cannot continue
a trend in n beyond the training range, and the neural network is seed-dependent at
large gaps, so a user who needs predictions at larger n should prefer the structured
model, or extend the training range with Clifford labels. (iii) Ideal simulation. We use a
noiseless statevector simulator, so noise-induced plateaus [10] are out of scope. (iv) The
label is an estimator. Each variance is estimated from 200 samples. We measured the
resulting label noise directly (mean absolute relabeling difference 0.021 in log10 units)
and it is small relative to the model error, but it is not zero. (v) Hidden axis patterns.



                                                  22
For random-Pauli circuits the drawn axis pattern is part of the circuit but not of the
feature vector. It contributes irreducible label variation, which we quantified as a noise
ceiling rather than hiding it. (vi) The cutoff. The barren cutoff y < −2 is a modeling
choice. The regression target is threshold-free, and Supplementary Table S3 shows the
classifier’s behavior at other cutoffs. (vii) Exact alternatives for this family. Because
every angle appears once with a Pauli generator, the gradient variance of these circuits
can be estimated exactly by Clifford sampling at any qubit count (Section 4.3), at
a cost of seconds per circuit. This weakens the argument that labels are expensive.
Three things remain in favor of a predictor: it costs microseconds per candidate, which
matters when the design space is searched; sampling cannot resolve variances far
below the reciprocal of the sample count, which is where barren plateaus live; and the
quadrature property fails as soon as parameters are shared between gates or generators
are not Paulis, as in problem-inspired ansätze, where a predictor trained on simulable
sizes has no such substitute. (viii) Gradient variance is not optimization outcome. The
label measures how flat the landscape is on average, not whether training succeeds.
As a check we trained several hundred fresh circuits from the same design space with
gradient descent and with Adam, with exact gradients and with gradients estimated
from 100 or 1 000 shots, for 200 steps from random starts. At n ≤ 12 between 94% and
99% of circuits reached the ground state of the observable regardless of their label,
and the rank correlation between the label and the loss decrease was between 0.08
and 0.19. At these sizes even the smallest gradient variances in the dataset correspond
to gradient components of order 10−2 , which a modest shot budget resolves. The
predictor should therefore be read as a screen for the gradient-variance scaling that
becomes prohibitive at larger n, not as a forecast of optimization success at the sizes
simulated here. (ix) Averaging over parameters. The mean over parameters of the
gradient variance is one summary of a circuit’s landscape. Other summaries, such
as the minimum over parameters or the distribution across layers, may matter for
particular optimizers. We release all per-circuit statistics (mean, median, minimum,
maximum, and the structural-zero count) so these can be studied.
    Two design choices protect the results. Seeds are fixed and reported, and the
extrapolation split is defined purely by qubit count, so no large-circuit information
leaks into training.

6 Conclusion
We studied how well the gradient variance of hardware-eﬀicient variational circuits
can be predicted from architecture alone. With the label defined as the mean over all
parameters and structural zeros removed, the answer is: almost completely. Gradient-
boosted trees, a neural network, and a structured linear model with one set of
coeﬀicients per categorical cell all reach R2 ≈ 0.980 on unseen architectures, against
a noise ceiling of 0.985 and 0.682 for a five-coeﬀicient rule in qubit count, depth,
and nominal cost locality. Part of the gap is explained by the effective weight of the
observable after the last entangling layer. Models with a continuous dependence on
qubit count extrapolate to 13- and 14-qubit circuits with R2 = 0.972 (neural network)
and 0.951 (structured linear); tree ensembles do not extrapolate. With exact Clifford



                                           23
labels up to 32 qubits, a model trained on at most 12 qubits remains accurate for
about eight more qubits; for local costs it then overestimates the decay, because the
causal cone saturates, and extending the training range to 20 qubits restores accu-
racy up to 32. Transfer to an entanglement pattern absent from training succeeds
when the pattern is described by its effect on the observable and the causal cone (R2
between 0.753 and 0.950). The predictor is a cheap screening tool for one circuit fam-
ily. Extending it to families with shared parameters, where no exact shortcut exists,
and to noisy circuits are natural next steps.
