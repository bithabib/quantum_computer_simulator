                                   Abstract
Barren plateaus are a central obstacle to training variational quantum circuits.
On a barren plateau the variance of the cost gradient shrinks exponentially with
the number of qubits, and gradient-based training stalls. The standard diagnostic
is to sample many gradients for every candidate circuit, which is exactly the cost
one would like to avoid when screening a large design space. We ask whether
a classical model can predict the empirical gradient variance of a hardware-
eﬀicient circuit from its architecture alone: qubit count, depth, rotation scheme,
entanglement pattern, entangler gate, and cost-observable locality. We build a
dataset of 20 000 random circuits with an exact statevector simulator. For each
circuit we compute the exact gradient of the cost with respect to every rotation
angle for 200 random parameter vectors, and we use the mean gradient variance
over all parameters as the label. Parameters whose gradient vanishes identically
for structural reasons are detected and excluded, so the label measures small but
non-zero gradients rather than exact symmetries. Because the sampled design
space contains only 5 168 distinct architectures, we evaluate with architecture-
grouped cross-validation, in which no architecture appears in both training and
test folds. Under this protocol a gradient-boosted regressor predicts log10 of the
label with R2 = 0.977 (mean over folds), against R2 = 0.682 for a physics-
informed linear model in qubit count, depth, and cost locality, and a within-
architecture noise ceiling of R2 ≈ 0.989. A classifier separates circuits below a
variance cutoff with an area under the ROC curve of 0.998. Trained on circuits
with at most 10 qubits, the regressor reaches R2 = 0.866 on unseen 11- and 12-
qubit circuits (0.950 for a neural network), with error broken down by qubit count
and cost locality. Replacing the categorical entanglement pattern by descriptors
of the entangling graph allows partial transfer to patterns absent from training,

     not a claim of quantum advantage and not a proof of barren-plateau scaling.

1 Introduction
Variational quantum algorithms (VQAs) are the leading way to use noisy
intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) hardware [1]. A VQA works in three steps.
It encodes a problem into a parameterized quantum circuit, called an ansatz. It
then measures a cost function C(θ) = ⟨ψ(θ)|O|ψ(θ)⟩. Finally, a classical optimizer
updates the parameters θ. This loop underlies variational quantum eigensolvers,
quantum approximate optimization, and most quantum machine-learning (QML)
classifiers [2–4].
     One problem stops this loop from scaling: the barren plateau (BP) [5, 6]. On
a barren plateau the variance of the cost gradient, taken over random parameter
settings, vanishes exponentially in the number of qubits. The loss landscape becomes
almost flat almost everywhere, and gradient-based training stalls. Several causes are
known. Global cost observables trigger barren plateaus [7]. So do highly expressive or
deep ansätze [8], poor initialization [9], and hardware noise [10]. Recent theory unifies
many of these results through the dynamical Lie algebra of the circuit [11, 12]. The
common thread is that a barren plateau is largely a property of the circuit design
rather than of one particular parameter setting.
     The usual way to check a circuit is to measure it. One samples many random
parameter vectors, evaluates the gradients, and estimates their variance. This is
exactly the cost one wants to avoid when screening a large design space, and it is hard-
est for many qubits, where barren plateaus matter most. This motivates our question.
Can a classical model predict the empirical gradient variance of a variational circuit
from its architecture alone, without sampling any gradients?
     We answer this question empirically for one widely used circuit family, the
hardware-eﬀicient ansatz [13]. We treat trainability prediction as a supervised learning
problem over circuit specifications. The features are cheap architectural descriptors:
small integers and categories. The label is the gradient variance, computed once,
offline, by exact statevector simulation. Throughout, we use two standard machine-
learning scores. The coeﬀicient of determination R2 is the fraction of label variance
a regressor explains; R2 = 1 is perfect and R2 = 0 is no better than predicting the
mean. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) is the prob-
ability that a classifier ranks a randomly chosen positive example above a randomly
chosen negative one; 0.5 is chance and 1 is perfect. We also report the mean absolute
error (MAE) of the regressor, in log10 units.


                                                2
    This article is a revised version of an earlier preprint. Two referee reports identified
two weaknesses in the original evidence, and the revision addresses both. First, the
original label was the variance of the gradient with respect to a single, fixed parameter.
Such a gradient can vanish identically for structural reasons, for example when the
rotation commutes with everything between it and the observable, and a structural
zero is not a barren plateau. The label is now the mean gradient variance over all
parameters, with structural zeros detected and excluded. Second, the original “held-
out” evaluation used a row-wise random split. Because the sampled design space
contains far fewer distinct architectures than rows, the same architecture routinely
appeared on both sides of that split. All headline numbers now come from architecture-
grouped cross-validation, and every learner is compared against simple controls that
encode what barren-plateau theory already predicts.
    The contributions of this article are as follows.
• We formulate barren-plateau screening as a supervised prediction task over one
  hardware-eﬀicient circuit family. The input is an architecture and the output is the
  log10 mean gradient variance over all parameters (regression), or a binary “barren”
  flag obtained by thresholding it (classification).
• We release a reproducible pipeline that generates a labeled dataset of 20 000 random
  circuits with an exact, dependency-light statevector simulator, using reverse-mode
  (adjoint) differentiation to obtain the exact gradient of every parameter, and that
  separates structural zeros from small non-zero gradients.
• We evaluate with architecture-grouped cross-validation and report row-wise random
  splitting only as a contrast. A gradient-boosted model reaches R2 = 0.977 under
  the grouped protocol, versus R2 = 0.682 for a physics-informed linear model and
  an estimated noise ceiling of R2 ≈ 0.989.
• We test transfer across scale. Trained only on n ≤ 10 qubits, the model attains
  R2 = 0.866 on unseen 11- and 12-qubit circuits. We break this down by qubit count
  and cost locality and report bootstrap confidence intervals, so the reader can see
  where the transfer holds and where it does not.
• We test transfer to entanglement patterns the model has never seen. Replacing the
  categorical pattern index with closed-form descriptors of the entangling graph makes
  such transfer possible, and a leave-one-pattern-out experiment over five patterns
  measures how much is lost.
• We replace permutation importance with a feature-ablation study under grouped
  cross-validation, which is robust to the strong dependence between the primitive
  and derived features. Cost locality and entanglement structure carry most of the
  predictive signal, in qualitative agreement with theory.
    We also want to be clear about scope. We make no claim of quantum advantage.
The model is classical and the data is classically simulated. The predicted quan-
tity is an empirical, finite-size proxy for trainability within one circuit family, not a
proof of exponential scaling. The contribution is a data-driven diagnostic for a known
quantum-computing bottleneck [14, 15].

2 Background and related work
2.1 Variational quantum circuits
We use a hardware-eﬀicient ansatz [13]. Each layer applies one single-qubit rotation
to every qubit, then a fixed pattern of two-qubit entangling gates. With n qubits and
L layers the state is

                        |ψ(θ)⟩ =         Uent         Raℓ,q (θℓ,q ) |0⟩⊗n ,          (1)

where Ra (θ) = exp(−iθσa /2) is a Pauli rotation about axis a ∈ {x, y, z} and Uent
is a layer of CZ or CX gates arranged linearly, circularly, or all-to-all (Section 3.1
defines these patterns precisely). The cost is the expectation of a Pauli-Z string,
C(θ) = ⟨ψ(θ)|O|ψ(θ)⟩, with either a local observable O = Z0 or a global one O =
Z0 Z1 · · · Zn−1 .

2.2 Barren plateaus
A circuit family has a barren plateau if Varθ [∂C/∂θk ] decays exponentially in n [5].
This is a statement about a family of circuits of growing size, not about any single
circuit. Cerezo et al. [7] proved that global cost functions cause barren plateaus even
at shallow depth, whereas local costs can stay trainable up to O(log n) depth. Cost
locality is therefore a first-order design knob. Expressibility [8] and entanglement also
correlate with flatter landscapes. Some structured architectures avoid the problem;
quantum convolutional networks are a proven example [16]. The dimension of the
dynamical Lie algebra generated by the circuit gives a unified, exact characterization
for many families [11, 12], and a recent review consolidates these results [6].
    Most of this work is analytical. It derives variance scaling laws for one ansatz
family at a time. Our work is empirical and complementary. We do not prove a
bound. Instead, we learn a predictive map across a heterogeneous but finite space of
architectures, at circuit sizes where the ground truth can be simulated exactly.

2.3 Exact gradients
For gates generated by an operator with two eigenvalues, the parameter-shift rule
gives the exact gradient from two circuit evaluations [17, 18]:

                         ∂C      [                             ]
                             = 12 C(θ + π2 ek ) − C(θ − π2 ek ) .                    (2)
                         ∂θk

Evaluating Eq. (2) for all nL parameters costs 2nL simulations per parameter vector.
On a classical simulator the same exact gradient vector is available from a single
backward pass. Reverse-mode (adjoint) differentiation walks the circuit backwards
once, maintaining the state and the adjoint state, and yields every ∂C/∂θk for roughly
the cost of three forward simulations. We use adjoint differentiation for the dataset

and verify it against Eq. (2) (Section 3.3). Each individual gradient is therefore exact
to floating-point precision. The label, a variance over finitely many random parameter
vectors, is nonetheless an estimator with its own sampling uncertainty, which we
quantify in Section 3.2.

2.4 Related data-driven approaches
Several lines of work use classical computation to rank or choose circuits before train-
ing. Quantum architecture search (QAS) optimizes the circuit structure itself, by
differentiable relaxation [19], by sampling from a supernet [20], or with training-free
proxies that score candidate circuits without optimizing them [21]. Expressibility and
entangling capability are classical descriptors that are estimated by sampling and
used to compare ansätze [22]. Lie-algebraic theory provides an exact, closed-form
trainability criterion for circuits whose generators are known [11, 12].
    Our work differs in three ways. The target is the empirical gradient variance itself,
not downstream task accuracy and not a proxy for it. The input is a handful of
architectural integers and categories, not a sampled statistic such as expressibility, so
a prediction costs microseconds. And the predictor is learned across a heterogeneous
design space and tested on architectures it has never seen, including larger qubit
counts. We are not aware of prior work that casts barren-plateau severity as a directly
predicted regression or classification target from cheap architectural features and that
also tests extrapolation across qubit count. We regard the QAS proxies and the Lie-
algebraic criteria as complementary: a learned predictor of the kind studied here could
serve as a zero-cost filter inside a QAS loop.

2.5 Trainability, simulability, and honest framing
Recent work urges caution about advantage claims. Cerezo et al. [23] argue that
the structure which removes barren plateaus often also makes a model classically
simulable. Bowles et al. [15] show that out-of-the-box classical models often match or
beat QML models on standard tasks. Schuld and Killoran [14] argue that the field
should de-emphasize “beating classical”. We adopt this stance. The object we predict
is a property of quantum circuits; the predictor and its value are entirely classical.

Rotation scheme.
With the fixed-ry scheme every rotation in Eq. (1) is Ry . With the random-Pauli
scheme the axis aℓ,q of each of the nL rotations is drawn independently and uniformly
from {x, y, z} once, when the spec is created, from the spec’s own random seed.
The axis pattern is then fixed for that spec: it does not change between the random
parameter vectors used to label the circuit. Two random-Pauli specs with the same
feature vector can therefore have different axis patterns. The feature vector does not
encode the pattern, so this variation acts as label noise from the model’s point of
view. We quantify its effect in Section 3.4.

Entanglement pattern.
Each layer applies the entangler gate to a fixed list of ordered qubit pairs (a, b), in
list order, after the rotation layer. The linear pattern uses the n − 1 nearest-neighbor
pairs (0, 1), (1, 2), . . . , (n − 2, n − 1). The circular pattern adds the wrap-around pair
(n − 1, 0), giving n gates per layer. The all-to-all pattern uses every pair (a, b) with


                                                  6
a < b, giving n(n − 1)/2 gates per layer. For CX the first qubit of each pair is the
control and the second is the target; CZ is symmetric. The same list is used in every
layer.

Cost observable.
The local cost measures Z on qubit 0 only. The global cost measures the parity
operator Z ⊗n .

3.2 Label generation
We label each spec in four steps. First, we draw 200 parameter vectors θ ∼
Unif[0, 2π)nL . Second, for each θ we compute the exact gradient vector ∇θ C by adjoint
differentiation. Third, for every parameter k we compute the population variance vk
of ∂C/∂θk over the 200 samples. Fourth, we detect structural zeros and average the
remaining variances.

Structural zeros.
Some rotations have a gradient that is identically zero for every θ. This happens when
the rotation commutes with everything between it and the observable: for example
an Rz applied immediately before a Z measurement on the same qubit, a rotation on
a qubit outside the light cone of a local observable in a shallow circuit, or a rotation
on the control qubit of CX gates that lead only to the observable’s support. Such a
parameter is untrainable, but the reason is an exact symmetry, not a barren plateau.
We flag parameter k as a structural zero when maxθ |∂C/∂θk | < 10−10 over all 200
samples. Section 3.3 verifies that flagged parameters remain exactly zero on fresh
samples, so the flag is a property of the circuit and not of the sample. At n ≤ 12 no
genuine barren-plateau variance in our data comes within eight orders of magnitude
of this tolerance, so the two populations do not overlap.

Sampling uncertainty of the label.
Each vk is an estimate from 200 samples. To measure the resulting label noise directly,
we relabeled 200 randomly chosen specs with an independent set of 200 parameter
vectors. The mean absolute difference between the two labels was 0.021 in log10 units,
and the largest difference was 0.20. This is small compared with the label’s spread
across architectures (standard deviation 0.94) and with the model errors reported
below.

Comparison with the single-parameter label.
The original version of this work used the variance of a single parameter, the rotation
on qubit 0 in layer ⌊L/2⌋, estimated by parameter shift. We keep that quantity as a
secondary column. Because the new dataset was generated from the same root seed,
it contains the same 20 000 specs and the same parameter samples as the original,
and the secondary column reproduces the original labels to within 4 × 10−15 on every
one of the 5 168 architectures; the only thing that changed is the label definition.
In 9.6% of specs it is a structural zero, and every such case in the original dataset
was clipped to a floor and labeled barren. Figure 3 compares the two labels. Where
the single-parameter gradient is not a structural zero, the two labels agree closely
(correlation 0.963, mean absolute difference 0.16). Where it is a structural zero, 69%
of the circuits are in fact trainable by the all-parameter label. The single-parameter
label therefore mixed two different phenomena, exactly as one referee anticipated, and
the all-parameter label removes the mixture.

3.3 Simulator and validation
Statevectors are evolved exactly with a small in-house NumPy simulator (qsim in the
released code), which applies each gate as a tensor contraction on the 2n -amplitude
state. We validate it in three ways, reproducible with one command in the repository.
(i) On 200 random circuits with up to seven qubits, the statevector agrees with an
independent dense-matrix simulation that builds the full 2n × 2n unitary of every
gate, to a maximum amplitude difference of 3.1e-16. (ii) On 200 random circuits, the
adjoint gradient agrees with the parameter-shift rule of Eq. (2) for every one of 4 792
parameters, to a maximum difference of 1.0e-15. (iii) Every parameter flagged as a
structural zero from 50 samples remained below 10−10 on 200 fresh samples, in all
1153 flagged cases.

3.4 Dataset and data description
We sample 20 000 specs. Each design parameter is drawn independently and uniformly
over the ranges in Table 2: n uniformly on {2, . . . , 12}, L uniformly on {1, . . . , 20},
and each categorical choice uniformly over its options. Every spec is labeled with 200
gradient samples. Generation is embarrassingly parallel; every spec is seeded repro-
ducibly from a root seed, and completed rows are appended to a comma-separated
text file (CSV) as they finish, so a long run can be resumed.

Architectures versus rows.
The design space has only 5 280 distinct feature vectors, and the 20 000 rows cover
5 168 of them, with a median of 4 and a maximum of 13 rows per architecture. Rows
sharing an architecture differ only in their random-Pauli axis pattern (if applicable)
and in the parameter samples used to label them. Two consequences follow. A row-
wise random split places 95.0% of test rows next to a training row with the identical
feature vector, so it measures interpolation within known architectures rather than
generalization to new ones; we therefore evaluate with architecture-grouped splits
(Section 3.5). And the within-architecture spread of the label is irreducible for any
model that sees only the feature vector. The mean within-architecture variance of
y is 0.010 against a total variance of 0.885, which caps the achievable R2 at about
0.989 overall: 1.000 for fixed-ry circuits, whose only within-architecture variation is
sampling noise, and 0.979 for random-Pauli circuits, whose hidden axis pattern adds
real variation.

Structural zeros are common: 17.0% of all parameters are structural zeros, and 87.0%

Architecture-grouped cross-validation.
Our primary protocol is K-fold cross-validation with K = 5 in which the folds are
formed from whole architectures. Every row is assigned to one fold according to its
feature vector, so no architecture appears in both the training and the test fold. Each
model is trained on K − 1 folds and scored on the remaining fold, and every row is
scored exactly once. We repeat the whole procedure 2 times with a different random
assignment of architectures to folds, giving 10 scores per model, and report their mean
and standard deviation. The standard deviation measures split-to-split variability.

Row-wise random split (for contrast only).
We also report the protocol of the original preprint, a single random split of rows
with 20% held out. As explained above, this split leaks architectures and its scores
are upper bounds on generalization.

Extrapolation across qubit count.
We train on all circuits with n ≤ 10 and test on all circuits with n ∈ {11, 12}. This
split is defined purely by qubit count, so no large-circuit information enters training.
We report scores for the whole test set and separately for n = 11, n = 12, local cost,
and global cost, with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals from 1000 resamples of the
test rows, and the spread over five random seeds of the HGB model. The controls of
Eq. (4) are fitted on the same training set; the physics-informed model can extrapolate
in n by construction, so it is the natural yardstick here.

Feature ablation.
To ask which architectural properties carry the signal, we retrain HGB under the
grouped protocol with a group of features removed and report the drop in R2 . This
avoids the known failure of permutation importance when features are strongly depen-
dent, as ours are: nL, L/n, and the entangler count are deterministic functions of the
primitive features.

Transfer to unseen entanglement patterns.
A categorical pattern index cannot be evaluated on a pattern the model has never
seen, because the index carries no information about what the new pattern is. To

ask whether the predictor transfers beyond the three sampled patterns, we replace
the index by four closed-form descriptors of the entangling graph, which are defined
for any pattern: the mean number of entangling gates per layer, the edge density of
the union graph over layers (edges divided by n(n − 1)/2), its maximum degree, and
its diameter (n if disconnected). We then generate ?? additional circuits with two
further patterns that are structurally different from the first three. Brickwork applies
the even nearest-neighbor pairs (0, 1), (2, 3), . . . on even-numbered layers and the odd
pairs (1, 2), (3, 4), . . . on odd-numbered layers, so each layer has about n/2 gates and
the union graph is the chain. Star applies (0, q) for q = 1, . . . , n − 1 in every layer, so
qubit 0 has degree n−1 and the diameter is 2. Everything else is sampled as in Table 2.
For each of the five patterns we train HGB on the descriptor features using the other
four patterns only and test on the held-out one (leave-one-pattern-out, LOPO). We
compare with the same model and features when the pattern is included in training
(architecture-grouped cross-validation on the pooled data, scored on that pattern’s
rows) and with the physics-informed linear control.
    All models use fixed random seeds, so the reported numbers reproduce exactly. All
code, command lines, seeds, and software versions are released; see the data availability
statement.

4 Results
4.1 Generalization to unseen architectures
Table 6 reports architecture-grouped cross-validation for all six learners and the three
controls. HGB gives R2 = 0.977 ± 0.003 and MAE 0.076 ± 0.004 on regression, and
AUC 0.998 ± 0.000 with accuracy 0.980 ± 0.003 on classification. The four non-linear
learners with enough capacity (HGB, the MLP, random forests, and extra trees) are
statistically indistinguishable on this protocol, with R2 between 0.974 and 0.978; k-
NN is close behind and the linear baseline is far behind (R2 = 0.701). We keep HGB
as the reference model because it is deterministic, fast to train, and needs no feature
scaling. Figure 4 shows its out-of-fold predictions.
    Three comparisons put these numbers in context. First, the controls. The physics-
informed linear model of Eq. (4) reaches R2 = 0.682 and the cost-locality subgroup
mean reaches R2 = 0.043. The learned model therefore explains 0.29 more of the label
variance than the known scaling rules alone, which is the part of the signal that this
work adds. Second, the ceiling. The within-architecture noise estimate of Section 3.4
caps R2 near 0.989, so HGB recovers roughly 99% of the explainable variance. Third,
the old protocol. The same HGB model scored on the row-wise random split of the
original preprint reaches R2 = 0.979 and AUC 0.999, essentially the same as under
the grouped protocol. This is not because the leakage was harmless in the original
work; it is because the corrected label is close to a deterministic function of the feature
vector (noise ceiling 0.989), so seeing a duplicate of an architecture adds little beyond
seeing its neighbors. With the original single-parameter label, whose structural zeros
were unpredictable from the features, the leakage did matter. One caveat applies to
the grouped protocol itself. The design space is a dense grid (5 168 of 5 280 cells are
populated), so a held-out architecture always has close neighbors in training, differing

4.3 Trainability versus qubit count
Figure 5 shows the mean label as a function of qubit count, split by cost locality.
Global-cost circuits show a steep, monotone decay of the mean gradient variance with
qubit number; local-cost circuits decay far more slowly. The shaded bands are one
standard deviation of the label across the heterogeneous circuits at each n, not the
uncertainty of the mean, which is much smaller. The dataset therefore contains the
qubit-scaling behavior that the predictor must learn, within the finite range n ≤ 12
where it can be measured exactly.

4.5 What drives trainability
Table 10 and Figure 6 report the feature ablation. Removing the three derived features
(keeping only the six design parameters) changes R2 by -0.002, so the derived fea-
tures add essentially nothing that the trees cannot reconstruct. Among the primitive
groups, removing cost locality costs the most (∆R2 = 0.342), followed by entangler
gate (∆R2 = 0.183) and then entangle pattern (∆R2 = 0.051). Removing all size
information at once (n, L, nL, L/n) costs 0.274, whereas removing n or L alone costs
nothing, because the derived features encode them; this is exactly the redundancy
that makes single-feature permutation importance unreliable here, and it is why we
report groups. This ordering is consistent with the qualitative predictions of barren-
plateau theory: global costs [7] and denser entanglement [8] drive circuits toward flat
landscapes. We stress the limits of this claim. The ablation shows that the model
relies on the same design variables that theory identifies, which is a consistency check
on the model, not an independent rediscovery of the theory; the theory was, after all,
used to choose the feature set.

5 Discussion
Our results support one main claim, stated within its scope. For the hardware-eﬀicient
family studied here, the mean gradient variance carries an architectural signature that
a cheap classical model can recover without any gradient sampling, and that signature


                                                                              18
goes beyond what the known cost-locality and qubit-count rules explain on their own.
The learned model captures about 99% of the explainable variance under a protocol
in which it never sees the test architectures.
    This suggests a practical workflow. First, screen a large space of candidate ansätze
with the learned model, at microseconds per candidate. Then reserve expensive
gradient-variance estimation for the shortlist it flags as trainable. Within a QAS loop,
the predictor could act as a zero-cost filter ahead of any training-free proxy or actual
training.
    Several limitations scope these claims. (i) One circuit family. The design space is
one hardware-eﬀicient ansatz with five fixed entanglement patterns. Section 4.6 shows
that graph descriptors allow partial transfer to a pattern the model has never seen, but
with a measurable loss, so predictions for a new gate pattern should be supported by
some labeled circuits of that pattern. The pipeline generates them from one command.
Predictions outside the hardware-eﬀicient family (for example, problem-inspired or
Hamiltonian-variational ansätze) are unsupported. (ii) Finite size. Labels exist only
at n ≤ 12. A barren plateau is a statement about scaling as n → ∞; our label
is a finite-size proxy, and the extrapolation result covers two additional qubits, not
an asymptotic regime. Tree ensembles in particular cannot continue a trend in n
beyond the training range, so a user who needs predictions at larger n should prefer
the MLP or a model with an explicit scaling term. (iii) Ideal simulation. We use a
noiseless statevector simulator, so noise-induced plateaus [10] are out of scope. (iv) The
label is an estimator. Each variance is estimated from 200 samples. We measured
the resulting label noise directly (mean absolute relabeling difference 0.021 in log10
units) and it is small relative to the model error, but it is not zero. (v) Hidden
axis patterns. For random-Pauli circuits the drawn axis pattern is part of the circuit
but not of the feature vector. It contributes irreducible label variation, which we
quantified as a noise ceiling rather than hiding it. (vi) The cutoff. The barren cutoff
y < −2 is a modeling choice. The regression target is threshold-free, and Table 9
shows the classifier’s behavior at other cutoffs. (vii) Averaging over parameters. The
mean over parameters of the gradient variance is one summary of a circuit’s landscape.
Other summaries, such as the minimum over parameters or the distribution across
layers, may matter for particular optimizers. We release all per-circuit statistics (mean,
median, minimum, maximum, and the structural-zero count) so these can be studied.
    Two design choices protect the results. Seeds are fixed and reported, and the
extrapolation split is defined purely by qubit count, so no large-circuit information
leaks into training.

6 Conclusion
We recast barren-plateau screening for hardware-eﬀicient circuits as supervised pre-
diction from architecture. Relative to the original preprint, this revision changes the
label to the mean gradient variance over all parameters with structural zeros removed,
evaluates only on architectures the model has never seen, and measures the learned
model against controls that encode the known theory. The corrected numbers are,




                                           19
perhaps surprisingly, higher than those first reported: the structural zeros of the orig-
inal label were the main source of unexplained variance, and once they are separated
from genuine small gradients the architecture determines the label almost completely.
These are the numbers we stand behind. A gradient-boosted model predicts log10 of
the mean gradient variance with R2 = 0.977 on unseen architectures, against 0.682
for a physics-informed linear model, and transfers to 11- and 12-qubit circuits with
R2 = 0.866 (0.950 for a neural network, which extrapolates the decay in n where
trees cannot). Cost locality and entanglement structure carry most of the signal, and
transfer to an entanglement pattern never seen in training is possible but lossy. The
model is a cheap screening tool for one circuit family; extending it to other families,
to noisy simulation, and to larger qubit counts via tensor-network labels are natural
next steps.
