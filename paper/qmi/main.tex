\title[Predicting the Trainability of Hardware-Efficient Variational Quantum Circuits]{Predicting
the Trainability of Hardware-Efficient Variational Quantum Circuits: A
Data-Driven Model for Barren Plateaus}

\author*[1]{\fnm{Md Habibur} \sur{Rahman}}\email{habib@gnu.ac.kr}

\affil*[1]{\orgdiv{Department of AI Convergence Engineering},
\orgname{Gyeongsang National University}, \orgaddress{\city{Jinju},
\country{Republic of Korea}}}

\abstract{Barren plateaus are a central obstacle to training variational
quantum circuits. On a barren plateau the variance of the cost gradient
shrinks exponentially with the number of qubits, and gradient-based training
stalls. The standard diagnostic is to sample many gradients for every
candidate circuit, which is exactly the cost one would like to avoid when
screening a large design space. We ask whether a classical model can predict
the empirical gradient variance of a hardware-efficient circuit from its
architecture alone: qubit count, depth, rotation scheme, entanglement pattern,
entangler gate, and cost-observable locality. We build a dataset of
\Nrows{} random circuits with an exact statevector simulator. For each circuit
we compute the exact gradient of the cost with respect to every rotation angle
for \Samples{} random parameter vectors, and we use the mean gradient variance
over all parameters as the label. Parameters whose gradient vanishes
identically for structural reasons are detected and excluded, so the label
measures small but non-zero gradients rather than exact symmetries. Because
the sampled design space contains only \Narch{} distinct architectures, we
evaluate with architecture-grouped cross-validation, in which no architecture
appears in both training and test folds. Under this protocol a
gradient-boosted regressor predicts $\log_{10}$ of the label with
$R^2=\RsqCV$ (mean over folds), against $R^2=\RsqPhys$ for a physics-informed
linear model in qubit count, depth, and cost locality, and a within-architecture
noise ceiling of $R^2\approx\RsqCeiling$. A classifier separates circuits
below a variance cutoff with an area under the ROC curve of \AucCV. Trained on
circuits with at most \ExtrapCut{} qubits, the regressor reaches
$R^2=\RsqExtrap$ on unseen 11- and 12-qubit circuits (\RsqExtrapMLP{} for a
neural network), with error broken down by qubit count and cost locality;
trained on all circuits with at most 12 qubits, the neural network reaches
$R^2=\LargeRsqMLP$ on a separate set of \LargeN{} circuits with 13 and 14
qubits.
Replacing the categorical entanglement pattern by descriptors of the
entangling graph allows partial transfer to patterns absent from training,
which we quantify with a leave-one-pattern-out experiment over five
patterns. A feature-ablation study confirms that cost
locality and entanglement structure carry most of the signal, in qualitative
agreement with barren-plateau theory. The contribution is an inexpensive,
classical screening tool for one circuit family, not a claim of quantum
advantage and not a proof of barren-plateau scaling.}

\keywords{Barren plateaus, Variational quantum circuits, Quantum machine
learning, Trainability, Gradient variance, Statevector simulation, Gradient
boosting}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Variational quantum algorithms (VQAs) are the leading way to use noisy
intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) hardware~\cite{cerezo2021vqa}. A VQA works in
three steps. It encodes a problem into a parameterized quantum circuit, called
an ansatz. It then measures a cost function
$C(\theta)=\langle\psi(\theta)|O|\psi(\theta)\rangle$. Finally, a classical
optimizer updates the parameters $\theta$. This loop underlies variational
quantum eigensolvers, quantum approximate optimization, and most quantum
machine-learning (QML) classifiers~\cite{havlicek2019supervised,
farhi2018classification,schuld2020circuit}.

One problem stops this loop from scaling: the barren plateau
(BP)~\cite{mcclean2018barren,larocca2025barren}. On a barren plateau the
variance of the cost gradient, taken over random parameter settings, vanishes
exponentially in the number of qubits. The loss landscape becomes almost flat
almost everywhere, and gradient-based training stalls. Several causes are
known. Global cost observables trigger barren plateaus~\cite{cerezo2021cost}.
So do highly expressive or deep ans\"atze~\cite{holmes2022expressibility},
poor initialization~\cite{grant2019initialization}, and hardware
noise~\cite{wang2021noise}. Recent theory unifies many of these results
through the dynamical Lie algebra of the
circuit~\cite{ragone2024lie,fontana2024adjoint}. The common thread is that a
barren plateau is largely a property of the circuit design rather than of one
particular parameter setting.

The usual way to check a circuit is to measure it. One samples many random
parameter vectors, evaluates the gradients, and estimates their variance. This
is exactly the cost one wants to avoid when screening a large design space, and
it is hardest for many qubits, where barren plateaus matter most. This
motivates our question. Can a classical model predict the empirical gradient
variance of a variational circuit from its architecture alone, without
sampling any gradients?

We answer this question empirically for one widely used circuit family, the
hardware-efficient ansatz~\cite{kandala2017hardware}. We treat trainability
prediction as a supervised learning problem over circuit specifications. The
features are cheap architectural descriptors: small integers and categories.
The label is the gradient variance, computed once, offline, by exact
statevector simulation. Throughout, we use two standard machine-learning
scores. The coefficient of determination $R^2$ is the fraction of label
variance a regressor explains; $R^2=1$ is perfect and $R^2=0$ is no better than
predicting the mean. The area under the receiver operating characteristic
curve (AUC) is the probability that a classifier ranks a randomly chosen
positive example above a randomly chosen negative one; $0.5$ is chance and
$1$ is perfect. We also report the mean absolute error (MAE) of the
regressor, in $\log_{10}$ units.

This article is a revised version of an earlier preprint. Two referee reports
identified two weaknesses in the original evidence, and the revision addresses
both. First, the original label was the variance of the gradient with respect
to a single, fixed parameter. Such a gradient can vanish identically for
structural reasons, for example when the rotation commutes with everything
between it and the observable, and a structural zero is not a barren plateau.
The label is now the mean gradient variance over all parameters, with
structural zeros detected and excluded. Second, the original ``held-out''
evaluation used a row-wise random split. Because the sampled design space
contains far fewer distinct architectures than rows, the same architecture
routinely appeared on both sides of that split. All headline numbers now come
from architecture-grouped cross-validation, and every learner is compared
against simple controls that encode what barren-plateau theory already
predicts.

The contributions of this article are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
\item We formulate barren-plateau screening as a supervised prediction task
over one hardware-efficient circuit family. The input is an architecture and
the output is the $\log_{10}$ mean gradient variance over all parameters
(regression), or a binary ``barren'' flag obtained by thresholding it
(classification).
\item We release a reproducible pipeline that generates a labeled dataset of
\Nrows{} random circuits with an exact, dependency-light statevector
simulator, using reverse-mode (adjoint) differentiation to obtain the exact
gradient of every parameter, and that separates structural zeros from small
non-zero gradients.
\item We evaluate with architecture-grouped cross-validation and report
row-wise random splitting only as a contrast. A gradient-boosted model reaches
$R^2=\RsqCV$ under the grouped protocol, versus $R^2=\RsqPhys$ for a
physics-informed linear model and an estimated noise ceiling of
$R^2\approx\RsqCeiling$.
\item We test transfer across scale. Trained only on $n\le\ExtrapCut$
qubits, the model attains $R^2=\RsqExtrap$ on unseen 11- and 12-qubit
circuits. We break this down by qubit count and cost locality and report
bootstrap confidence intervals, so the reader can see where the transfer
holds and where it does not.
\item We test transfer to entanglement patterns the model has never seen.
Replacing the categorical pattern index with closed-form descriptors of the
entangling graph makes such transfer possible, and a leave-one-pattern-out
experiment over five patterns measures how much is lost.
\item We replace permutation importance with a feature-ablation study under
grouped cross-validation, which is robust to the strong dependence between
the primitive and derived features. Cost locality and entanglement structure
carry most of the predictive signal, in qualitative agreement with theory.
\end{itemize}

We also want to be clear about scope. We make no claim of quantum advantage.
The model is classical and the data is classically simulated. The predicted
quantity is an empirical, finite-size proxy for trainability within one circuit
family, not a proof of exponential scaling. The contribution is a data-driven
diagnostic for a known quantum-computing
bottleneck~\cite{schuld2022killoran,bowles2024benchmarking}.

\section{Background and related work}\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Variational quantum circuits}
We use a hardware-efficient ansatz~\cite{kandala2017hardware}. Each layer
applies one single-qubit rotation to every qubit, then a fixed pattern of
two-qubit entangling gates. With $n$ qubits and $L$ layers the state is
\begin{equation}
|\psi(\theta)\rangle = \prod_{\ell=1}^{L}
  \Big[ U_{\mathrm{ent}} \prod_{q=1}^{n} R_{a_{\ell,q}}(\theta_{\ell,q}) \Big]
  |0\rangle^{\otimes n},
\label{eq:ansatz}
\end{equation}
where $R_a(\theta)=\exp(-i\theta\sigma_a/2)$ is a Pauli rotation about axis
$a\in\{x,y,z\}$ and $U_{\mathrm{ent}}$ is a layer of CZ or CX gates arranged
linearly, circularly, or all-to-all (Section~\ref{sec:spec} defines these
patterns precisely). The cost is the expectation of a Pauli-$Z$ string,
$C(\theta)=\langle\psi(\theta)|O|\psi(\theta)\rangle$, with either a local
observable $O=Z_0$ or a global one $O=Z_0Z_1\cdots Z_{n-1}$.

\subsection{Barren plateaus}
A circuit family has a barren plateau if
$\mathrm{Var}_\theta[\partial C/\partial\theta_k]$ decays exponentially in
$n$~\cite{mcclean2018barren}. This is a statement about a family of circuits
of growing size, not about any single circuit. Cerezo et
al.~\cite{cerezo2021cost} proved that global cost functions cause barren
plateaus even at shallow depth, whereas local costs can stay trainable up to
$O(\log n)$ depth. Cost locality is therefore a first-order design knob.
Expressibility~\cite{holmes2022expressibility} and entanglement also correlate
with flatter landscapes. Some structured architectures avoid the problem;
quantum convolutional networks are a proven
example~\cite{pesah2021absence}. The dimension of the dynamical Lie algebra
generated by the circuit gives a unified, exact characterization for many
families~\cite{ragone2024lie,fontana2024adjoint}, and a recent review
consolidates these results~\cite{larocca2025barren}.

Most of this work is analytical. It derives variance scaling laws for one
ansatz family at a time. Our work is empirical and complementary. We do not
prove a bound. Instead, we learn a predictive map across a heterogeneous but
finite space of architectures, at circuit sizes where the ground truth can be
simulated exactly.

\subsection{Exact gradients}\label{sec:gradients}
For gates generated by an operator with two eigenvalues, the parameter-shift
rule gives the exact gradient from two circuit
evaluations~\cite{mitarai2018circuit,schuld2019param}:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial C}{\partial\theta_k}
  = \tfrac{1}{2}\big[C(\theta + \tfrac{\pi}{2}e_k)
  - C(\theta - \tfrac{\pi}{2}e_k)\big].
\label{eq:pshift}
\end{equation}
Evaluating Eq.~\eqref{eq:pshift} for all $nL$ parameters costs $2nL$
simulations per parameter vector. On a classical simulator the same exact
gradient vector is available from a single backward pass. Reverse-mode
(adjoint) differentiation walks the circuit backwards once, maintaining the
state and the adjoint state, and yields every $\partial C/\partial\theta_k$ for
roughly the cost of three forward simulations. We use adjoint differentiation
for the dataset and verify it against Eq.~\eqref{eq:pshift}
(Section~\ref{sec:validation}). Each individual gradient is therefore exact
to floating-point precision. The label, a variance over finitely many
random parameter vectors, is nonetheless an estimator with its own sampling
uncertainty, which we quantify in Section~\ref{sec:label}.

\subsection{Related data-driven approaches}\label{sec:related}
Several lines of work use classical computation to rank or choose circuits
before training. Quantum architecture search (QAS) optimizes the circuit
structure itself, by differentiable relaxation~\cite{zhang2022dqas},
by sampling from a supernet~\cite{du2022qas}, or with training-free proxies
that score candidate circuits without optimizing
them~\cite{he2024tfqas}. Expressibility and entangling capability are
classical descriptors that are estimated by sampling and used to compare
ans\"atze~\cite{sim2019expressibility}. Lie-algebraic theory provides an
exact, closed-form trainability criterion for circuits whose generators are
known~\cite{ragone2024lie,fontana2024adjoint}.

Our work differs in three ways. The target is the empirical gradient variance
itself, not downstream task accuracy and not a proxy for it. The input is a
handful of architectural integers and categories, not a sampled statistic
such as expressibility, so a prediction costs microseconds. And the predictor
is learned across a heterogeneous design space and tested on architectures it
has never seen, including larger qubit counts. We are not aware of prior work
that casts barren-plateau severity as a directly predicted regression or
classification target from cheap architectural features and that also tests
extrapolation across qubit count. We regard the QAS proxies and the
Lie-algebraic criteria as complementary: a learned predictor of the kind
studied here could serve as a zero-cost filter inside a QAS loop.

\subsection{Trainability, simulability, and honest framing}
Recent work urges caution about advantage claims. Cerezo et
al.~\cite{cerezo2025simulability} argue that the structure which removes
barren plateaus often also makes a model classically simulable. Bowles et
al.~\cite{bowles2024benchmarking} show that out-of-the-box classical models
often match or beat QML models on standard tasks. Schuld and
Killoran~\cite{schuld2022killoran} argue that the field should de-emphasize
``beating classical''. We adopt this stance. The object we predict is a
property of quantum circuits; the predictor and its value are entirely
classical.

\subsection{Circuit specification and feature space}\label{sec:spec}
Each datapoint is a specification, or spec. A spec fixes one circuit family
through six design parameters: the qubit count $n$, the layer count $L$, the
rotation scheme, the entanglement pattern, the entangler gate, and the cost
observable. Table~\ref{tab:features} lists the nine-element feature vector we
hand to the model. Six entries are the design parameters themselves, which we
call the primitive features. Three are derived from them in closed form and
are included only for the convenience of the learner. No quantum simulation
is needed to obtain any feature.

\texttt{n\_layers}         & number of ansatz layers $L$ & primitive\\
\texttt{ansatz\_type}      & fixed-\texttt{ry} (0) versus random-Pauli axes (1) & primitive\\
\texttt{entangle\_pattern} & linear (0), circular (1), all-to-all (2) & primitive\\

\texttt{cost\_global}      & local $Z_0$ (0) versus global $Z^{\otimes n}$ (1) & primitive\\

\paragraph{Rotation scheme.} With the fixed-\texttt{ry} scheme every rotation
in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ansatz} is $R_y$. With the random-Pauli scheme the axis
$a_{\ell,q}$ of each of the $nL$ rotations is drawn independently and
uniformly from $\{x,y,z\}$ once, when the spec is created, from the spec's
own random seed. The axis pattern is then fixed for that spec: it does not
change between the random parameter vectors used to label the circuit. Two
random-Pauli specs with the same feature vector can therefore have different
axis patterns. The feature vector does not encode the pattern, so this
variation acts as label noise from the model's point of view. We quantify its
effect in Section~\ref{sec:data}.

\paragraph{Entanglement pattern.} Each layer applies the entangler gate to a
fixed list of ordered qubit pairs $(a,b)$, in list order, after the rotation
layer. The linear pattern uses the $n-1$ nearest-neighbor pairs
$(0,1),(1,2),\dots,(n-2,n-1)$. The circular pattern adds the wrap-around pair
$(n-1,0)$, giving $n$ gates per layer. The all-to-all pattern uses every pair
$(a,b)$ with $a<b$, giving $n(n-1)/2$ gates per layer. For CX the first
qubit of each pair is the control and the second is the target; CZ is
symmetric. The same list is used in every layer.

\paragraph{Cost observable.} The local cost measures $Z$ on qubit $0$ only.
The global cost measures the parity operator $Z^{\otimes n}$.

\subsection{Label generation}\label{sec:label}
We label each spec in four steps. First, we draw \Samples{} parameter vectors
$\theta\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,2\pi)^{nL}$. Second, for each $\theta$ we compute
the exact gradient vector $\nabla_\theta C$ by adjoint differentiation. Third,
for every parameter $k$ we compute the population variance $v_k$ of
$\partial C/\partial\theta_k$ over the \Samples{} samples. Fourth, we detect
structural zeros and average the remaining variances.

\paragraph{Structural zeros.} Some rotations have a gradient that is
identically zero for every $\theta$. This happens when the rotation commutes
with everything between it and the observable: for example an $R_z$ applied
immediately before a $Z$ measurement on the same qubit, a rotation on a
qubit outside the light cone of a local observable in a shallow circuit, or a
rotation on the control qubit of CX gates that lead only to the observable's
support. Such a parameter is untrainable, but the reason is an exact
symmetry, not a barren plateau. We flag parameter $k$ as a structural zero
when $\max_\theta|\partial C/\partial\theta_k|<10^{-10}$ over all \Samples{}
samples. Section~\ref{sec:validation} verifies that flagged parameters remain
exactly zero on fresh samples, so the flag is a property of the circuit and
not of the sample. At $n\le12$ no genuine barren-plateau variance in our data
comes within eight orders of magnitude of this tolerance, so the two
populations do not overlap.

\paragraph{Regression and classification labels.} Let $\mathcal{K}$ be the
set of parameters that are not structural zeros. The label is
\begin{equation}
y=\log_{10}\Vbar,\qquad
\Vbar=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{K}|}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}} v_k .
\label{eq:label}
\end{equation}
No floor or clipping is applied; $\Vbar$ is strictly positive by
construction, and the smallest value in the dataset is
$10^{\LabelMin}$. Specs with $\mathcal{K}=\emptyset$ (every parameter a
structural zero) cannot be labeled and are excluded; there are \NallStruct{}
such rows. The classification label is $\mathbb{1}[y<-2]$, which we call
``barren'' for brevity. The cutoff is a modeling choice: $\Vbar<10^{-2}$
means the typical gradient component has magnitude below $0.1$, which for a
Haar-random circuit corresponds to roughly seven qubits. The regression
target is threshold-free, and Section~\ref{sec:cutoff} reports the effect of
moving the cutoff.

\paragraph{Sampling uncertainty of the label.} Each $v_k$ is an estimate from
\Samples{} samples. To measure the resulting label noise directly, we
relabeled \RepeatN{} randomly chosen specs with an independent set of
\Samples{} parameter vectors. The mean absolute difference between the two
labels was \RepeatMAD{} in $\log_{10}$ units, and the largest difference was
\RepeatMax{}. This is small compared with the label's spread across
architectures (standard deviation \LabelStd) and with the model errors
reported below.

\paragraph{Comparison with the single-parameter label.} The original version
of this work used the variance of a single parameter, the rotation on qubit
$0$ in layer $\lfloor L/2\rfloor$, estimated by parameter shift. We keep that
quantity as a secondary column. Because the new dataset was generated from
the same root seed, it contains the same \Nrows{} specs and the same
parameter samples as the original, and the secondary column reproduces the
original labels to within $4\times10^{-15}$ on every one of the \Narch{}
architectures; the only thing that changed is the label definition. In \LegacyFloorFrac\% of specs it is a
structural zero, and every such case in the original dataset was clipped to a
floor and labeled barren. Figure~\ref{fig:labelcompare} compares the two
labels. Where the single-parameter gradient is not a structural zero, the two
labels agree closely (correlation \LegacyCorr, mean absolute difference
\LegacyMAD). Where it is a structural zero, \LegacyFloorTrainable\% of the
circuits are in fact trainable by the all-parameter label. The
single-parameter label therefore mixed two different phenomena, exactly as
one referee anticipated, and the all-parameter label removes the mixture.

\subsection{Simulator and validation}\label{sec:validation}
Statevectors are evolved exactly with a small in-house NumPy simulator
(\texttt{qsim} in the released code), which applies each gate as a tensor
contraction on the $2^n$-amplitude state. We validate it in three ways,
reproducible with one command in the repository. (i)~On \ValN{} random
circuits with up to seven qubits, the statevector agrees with an independent
dense-matrix simulation that builds the full $2^n\times2^n$ unitary of every
gate, to a maximum amplitude difference of \ValState. (ii)~On \ValN{} random
circuits, the adjoint gradient agrees with the parameter-shift rule of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:pshift} for every one of \ValGradN{} parameters, to a maximum
difference of \ValGrad. (iii)~Every parameter flagged as a structural zero
from 50 samples remained below $10^{-10}$ on 200 fresh samples, in all
\ValStructN{} flagged cases.

comma-separated text file (CSV) as they finish, so a long run can be resumed.

\subsection{Models and evaluation}\label{sec:eval}
We compare six learner families spanning linear, instance-based, neural, and
tree-ensemble inductive biases: a linear baseline (ridge regression or logistic
regression), $k$-nearest neighbors, a multilayer perceptron with two hidden
layers, random forests, extremely randomized trees, and histogram-based
gradient-boosted trees (HGB). All are scikit-learn
implementations~\cite{pedregosa2011scikit}; the histogram booster follows the
design of LightGBM~\cite{ke2017lightgbm}. Supplementary Table~S1 lists the hyperparameters. They were fixed a priori
and not tuned on the test folds. Features are standardized (zero mean, unit
variance on the training fold) for the linear, $k$-NN, and MLP models only.


\paragraph{Controls.} Because barren-plateau theory already predicts the
direction of the main effects, a learner should be judged against what those
known rules achieve on their own. We include three controls. The
\emph{training mean} predicts the mean label of the training fold. The
\emph{cost-locality subgroup mean} predicts the training-fold mean label
separately for local and global costs. The \emph{physics-informed linear
model} fits
\begin{equation}
\hat y=\beta_0+\beta_1 n+\beta_2 L+\beta_3\,\mathbb{1}_{\mathrm{global}}
  +\beta_4\, n\,\mathbb{1}_{\mathrm{global}},
\label{eq:phys}
\end{equation}
which encodes a linear-in-$n$ decay of $\log_{10}\Vbar$ whose slope depends on
cost locality, by ordinary least squares (or logistic regression on the same
four features for classification).

\paragraph{Architecture-grouped cross-validation.} Our primary protocol is
$K$-fold cross-validation with $K=\NFoldsK$ in which the folds are formed
from whole architectures. Every row is assigned to one fold according to its
feature vector, so no architecture appears in both the training and the test
fold. Each model is trained on $K-1$ folds and scored on the remaining fold,
and every row is scored exactly once. We repeat the whole procedure
\NRepeats{} times with a different random assignment of architectures to
folds, giving \NFolds{} scores per model, and report their mean and standard
deviation. The standard deviation measures split-to-split variability.

\paragraph{Row-wise random split (for contrast only).} We also report the
protocol of the original preprint, a single random split of rows with 20\%
held out. As explained above, this split leaks architectures and its scores
are upper bounds on generalization.

\paragraph{Extrapolation across qubit count.} We train on all circuits with
$n\le\ExtrapCut$ and test on all circuits with $n\in\{11,12\}$. This split is
defined purely by qubit count, so no large-circuit information enters
training. We report scores for the whole test set and separately for $n=11$,
$n=12$, local cost, and global cost, with 95\% bootstrap confidence intervals
from 1000 resamples of the test rows, and the spread over five random seeds of
the HGB model. The controls of Eq.~\eqref{eq:phys} are fitted on the same
training set; the physics-informed model can extrapolate in $n$ by
construction, so it is the natural yardstick here.

\paragraph{Feature ablation.} To ask which architectural properties carry the
signal, we retrain HGB under the grouped protocol with a group of features
removed and report the drop in $R^2$. This avoids the known failure of
permutation importance when features are strongly dependent, as ours are:
$nL$, $L/n$, and the entangler count are deterministic functions of the
primitive features.

\paragraph{Transfer to unseen entanglement patterns.} A categorical pattern
index cannot be evaluated on a pattern the model has never seen, because the
index carries no information about what the new pattern is. To ask whether
the predictor transfers beyond the three sampled patterns, we replace the
index by four closed-form descriptors of the entangling graph, which are
defined for any pattern: the mean number of entangling gates per layer, the
edge density of the union graph over layers (edges divided by $n(n-1)/2$),
its maximum degree, and its diameter ($n$ if disconnected). We then generate
\LopoExtraRows{} additional circuits with two further patterns that are
structurally different from the first three. \emph{Brickwork} applies the
even nearest-neighbor pairs $(0,1),(2,3),\dots$ on even-numbered layers and
the odd pairs $(1,2),(3,4),\dots$ on odd-numbered layers, so each layer has
about $n/2$ gates and the union graph is the chain. \emph{Star} applies
$(0,q)$ for $q=1,\dots,n-1$ in every layer, so qubit $0$ has degree $n-1$
and the diameter is $2$. Everything else is sampled as in
Table~\ref{tab:designspace}. For each of the five patterns we train HGB on
the descriptor features using the other four patterns only and test on the
held-out one (leave-one-pattern-out, LOPO). We compare with the same model
and features when the pattern is included in training (architecture-grouped
cross-validation on the pooled data, scored on that pattern's rows) and with
the physics-informed linear control.

All models use fixed random seeds, so the reported numbers reproduce exactly.
All code, command lines, seeds, and software versions are released; see the
data availability statement.

\subsection{Generalization to unseen architectures}\label{sec:cv}
Table~\ref{tab:models} reports architecture-grouped cross-validation for all
six learners and the three controls. HGB gives $R^2=\RsqCV\pm\RsqCVsd$ and
MAE $\MaeCV\pm\MaeCVsd$ on regression, and AUC $\AucCV\pm\AucCVsd$ with
accuracy $\AccCV\pm\AccCVsd$ on classification. The four non-linear
learners with enough capacity (HGB, the MLP, random forests, and extra trees)
are statistically indistinguishable on this protocol, with $R^2$ between
\RsqCVRandomForest{} and \RsqCVExtraTrees{}; $k$-NN is close behind and
the linear baseline is far behind ($R^2=\RsqCVLinearbaseline$). We keep HGB
as the reference model because it is deterministic, fast to train, and
needs no feature scaling. Figure~\ref{fig:predicted} shows its out-of-fold
predictions.

Three comparisons put these numbers in context. First, the controls. The
physics-informed linear model of Eq.~\eqref{eq:phys} reaches $R^2=\RsqPhys$
and the cost-locality subgroup mean reaches $R^2=\RsqSub$. The learned model
therefore explains \GainOverPhys{} more of the label variance than the
known scaling rules alone, which is the part of the signal that this work
adds. Second, the ceiling. The within-architecture noise estimate of
Section~\ref{sec:data} caps $R^2$ near \RsqCeiling, so HGB recovers roughly
\FracOfCeiling\% of the explainable variance. Third, the old protocol. The same
HGB model scored on the row-wise random split of the original preprint reaches
$R^2=\RowRsq$ and AUC \RowAuc, essentially the same as under the grouped
protocol. This is not because the leakage was harmless in the original work;
it is because the corrected label is close to a deterministic function of the
feature vector (noise ceiling \RsqCeiling), so seeing a duplicate of an
architecture adds little beyond seeing its neighbors. With the original
single-parameter label, whose structural zeros were unpredictable from the
features, the leakage did matter. One caveat applies to the grouped protocol
itself. The design space is a dense grid (\Narch{} of \DesignSpace{} cells
are populated), so a held-out architecture always has close neighbors in
training, differing by one qubit or one layer. The grouped score therefore
measures interpolation across a dense design space, not extrapolation;
Sections~\ref{sec:extrap} and \ref{sec:lopo} probe beyond it.

\subsection{Extrapolation across qubit count}\label{sec:extrap}
Table~\ref{tab:extrapbreak} (and Supplementary Table~S4 for all learners)
reports the qubit-count split, with
\ExtrapTrainRows{} training circuits at $n\le\ExtrapCut$ and
\ExtrapTestRows{} test circuits at $n\in\{11,12\}$. HGB attains
$R^2=\RsqExtrap$ (95\% CI $[\RsqExtrapLo,\RsqExtrapHi]$; seed spread
$\pm\RsqExtrapSeedSd$) and MAE \MaeExtrap{} (95\% CI
$[\MaeExtrapLo,\MaeExtrapHi]$). The physics-informed linear model, which can
extrapolate in $n$ by construction, reaches only $R^2=\RsqExtrapPhys$ and MAE
\MaeExtrapPhys: a single linear-in-$n$ slope per cost type, fitted on
$n\le\ExtrapCut$, does not describe the $n=11,12$ circuits well. The most
notable entry in Supplementary Table~S4 is the MLP, which reaches
$R^2=\RsqExtrapMLP$ (95\% CI $[\RsqExtrapMLPLo,\RsqExtrapMLPHi]$), well
above every tree ensemble. This is the expected behavior of the two model
classes: a tree ensemble's prediction is constant outside the training range
of a feature, so it cannot continue the decay in $n$ past $n=10$, whereas a
neural network with continuous inputs can. In-family the two are
indistinguishable; out of range the inductive bias decides.

Table~\ref{tab:extrapbreak} breaks the result down by qubit count and cost
locality. Error grows from $n=11$ to $n=12$ for both models, and global-cost
circuits at $n=12$ are the hardest subset (HGB $R^2=\RsqENOneTwoglobal$, MLP
\RsqEMNOneTwoglobal), as expected, since they are furthest from training in
both $n$ and label value. Every subset is predicted far better than the
subgroup-mean and physics-informed controls. The result should still be read
narrowly: small-circuit labels transfer usefully to the largest sizes we can
simulate exactly, two qubits beyond training. This is not evidence of
unbounded extrapolation, and Section~\ref{sec:discussion} lists it as a
limitation.

\paragraph{Widening the gap.} Figure~\ref{fig:gap} (Supplementary
Table~S5) repeats the experiment with the training cutoff moved down: train on
$n\le k$ and test on every larger circuit, for $k=6,\dots,10$. The two
model classes separate cleanly. The MLP degrades gracefully, from
$R^2=\GapMLPkTen$ at $k=10$ to \GapMLPkEight{} at $k=8$ and
\GapMLPkSix{} at $k=6$, where it is predicting 7- to 12-qubit circuits from
2- to 6-qubit ones. HGB falls from \GapHGBkTen{} to \GapHGBkEight{} and to
\GapHGBkSix{} at $k=6$, no better than the training mean, because its
prediction for any $n$ beyond the training range is the prediction for
$n=k$. The physics-informed linear model stays near \GapPhyskEight{}
regardless of $k$. Per qubit count (right panel, $k=8$), the MLP's $R^2$
declines smoothly with distance from the training range, which is the
behavior one wants from a screening tool that will be used beyond the
sizes it was trained on.

\subsection{Sensitivity to the barren cutoff}\label{sec:cutoff}
Supplementary Table~S3 moves the classification cutoff from $-1.5$ to $-3$,
which changes the barren fraction from \BarrenCutmOnepFive\% to
\BarrenCutmThreepZero\%. The AUC stays at or above \AucCutmOnepFive{} for

\subsection{What drives trainability}\label{sec:ablation}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} and Figure~\ref{fig:ablation} report the feature
ablation. Removing the three derived features (keeping only the six design
parameters) changes $R^2$ by \DeltaDerived, so the derived features add
essentially nothing that the trees cannot reconstruct. Among the primitive
groups, removing \AblTopOne{} costs the most ($\Delta R^2=\AblTopOneDelta$),
followed by \AblTopTwo{} ($\Delta R^2=\AblTopTwoDelta$) and then
\AblTopThree{} ($\Delta R^2=\AblTopThreeDelta$). Removing all size
information at once ($n$, $L$, $nL$, $L/n$) costs \DeltaSize, whereas
removing $n$ or $L$ alone costs nothing, because the derived features encode
them; this is exactly the redundancy that makes single-feature permutation
importance unreliable here, and it is why we report groups.
This ordering is consistent with the qualitative predictions of
barren-plateau theory: global costs~\cite{cerezo2021cost} and denser
entanglement~\cite{holmes2022expressibility} drive circuits toward flat
landscapes. We stress the limits of this claim. The ablation shows that the
model relies on the same design variables that theory identifies, which is a
consistency check on the model, not an independent rediscovery of the
theory; the theory was, after all, used to choose the feature set.

\subsection{Transfer to unseen entanglement patterns}\label{sec:lopo}
Table~\ref{tab:lopo} and Figure~\ref{fig:lopo} report the leave-one-pattern-out
experiment. With the graph descriptors in place of the pattern index, the
pooled five-pattern model loses nothing in-family (grouped-CV
$R^2=\LopoDescRsq$ with descriptors versus \LopoIndexRsq{} with the index).
When a pattern is held out entirely, the outcome depends on where its
descriptors fall relative to the training patterns. Brickwork, whose union
graph is the same chain as linear and whose other descriptors all lie
inside the range spanned by the training patterns, is predicted with
$R^2=\LopoBrickUnseen$
(\LopoBrickSeen{} when seen), far above the physics-informed control
(\LopoBrickPhys). Star (\LopoStarUnseen), linear (\LopoLinearUnseen), and
circular (\LopoCircularUnseen) are predicted with moderate accuracy, each a
little above the control, and with a systematic bias of about a quarter of a
$\log_{10}$ unit for linear and circular: the model interpolates between the
remaining patterns and lands slightly too high or too low. All-to-all, which
is the extreme of every descriptor (density $1$, maximum degree $n-1$,
diameter $1$), is predicted worst ($R^2=\LopoAllUnseen$) and worse than the
physics-informed control (\LopoAllPhys); a tree model cannot extrapolate to
a density it has never seen. The classifier is more forgiving: AUC stays
between \LopoCircularUnseenAuc{} and \LopoBrickUnseenAuc{} for every held-out
pattern, so the barren-or-not ranking transfers even where the regression
value does not.

Two readings follow. First, transfer is possible in principle: a pattern
whose descriptors lie inside the range spanned by the training patterns is
predicted well without a single labeled example of it. Second, transfer is
not free: every held-out pattern is predicted worse than a seen one, and a
pattern at the edge of the descriptor space fails. A user who needs
predictions for a new gate pattern should therefore add a modest number of
labeled circuits of that pattern to the training set, which the released
pipeline does from one command, rather than rely on zero-shot transfer.

\subsection{How much data is needed}\label{sec:learningcurve}
Supplementary Figure~S1 shows grouped-CV $R^2$ as a function of the
number of training rows, with whole architectures added at a time. Both
models reach $R^2>0.9$ with about \LCRowsNinety{} rows and are within
$0.02$ of their final value by \LCRowsSaturate{} rows; the MLP is the more
data-efficient of the two below 2\,000 rows (\LCMLPFiveHundred{} versus
\LCHGBFiveHundred{} at 500 rows). For a user extending the predictor to a
new circuit family, this sets the expectation: a few thousand labeled
circuits, which the released generator produces in well under an hour on a
laptop for $n\le12$, suffice to reach the plateau.


\subsection{Screening use case}\label{sec:screening}

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

Our results support one main claim, stated within its scope. For the
hardware-efficient family studied here, the mean gradient variance carries an
architectural signature that a cheap classical model can recover without any
gradient sampling, and that signature goes beyond what the known
cost-locality and qubit-count rules explain on their own. The learned model
captures about \FracOfCeiling\% of the explainable variance under a protocol
in which it never sees the test architectures.

This suggests a practical workflow. First, screen a large space of candidate
ans\"atze with the learned model, at microseconds per candidate. Then reserve
expensive gradient-variance estimation for the shortlist it flags as
trainable. Section~\ref{sec:screening} shows that, within the design space
studied, the shortlist is clean: the top fifth of the ranking contains no
barren circuit. Within a QAS loop, the predictor could act as a zero-cost filter
ahead of any training-free proxy or actual training.

Several limitations scope these claims.
(i)~\emph{One circuit family.} The design space is one hardware-efficient
ansatz with five fixed entanglement patterns. Section~\ref{sec:lopo} shows
that graph descriptors allow partial transfer to a pattern the model has
never seen, but with a measurable loss, so predictions for a new gate
pattern should be supported by some labeled circuits of that pattern. The
pipeline generates them from one command. Predictions outside the
hardware-efficient family (for example, problem-inspired or
Hamiltonian-variational ans\"atze) are unsupported.
(ii)~\emph{Finite size.} Labels exist only at $n\le12$. A barren plateau is a
statement about scaling as $n\to\infty$; our label is a finite-size proxy,
and the extrapolation result covers two additional qubits, not an asymptotic
regime. Tree ensembles in particular cannot continue a trend in $n$ beyond
the training range, so a user who needs predictions at larger $n$ should
prefer the MLP or a model with an explicit scaling term.
(iii)~\emph{Ideal simulation.} We use a noiseless statevector simulator, so
noise-induced plateaus~\cite{wang2021noise} are out of scope.
(iv)~\emph{The label is an estimator.} Each variance is estimated from
\Samples{} samples. We measured the resulting label noise directly (mean
absolute relabeling difference \RepeatMAD{} in $\log_{10}$ units) and it is
small relative to the model error, but it is not zero.
(v)~\emph{Hidden axis patterns.} For random-Pauli circuits the drawn axis
pattern is part of the circuit but not of the feature vector. It contributes
irreducible label variation, which we quantified as a noise ceiling rather
than hiding it.
(vi)~\emph{The cutoff.} The barren cutoff $y<-2$ is a modeling choice. The
regression target is threshold-free, and Supplementary Table~S3 shows the
classifier's behavior at other cutoffs.
(vii)~\emph{Averaging over parameters.} The mean over parameters of the
gradient variance is one summary of a circuit's landscape. Other summaries,
such as the minimum over parameters or the distribution across layers, may
matter for particular optimizers. We release all per-circuit statistics
(mean, median, minimum, maximum, and the structural-zero count) so these can
be studied.

Two design choices protect the results. Seeds are fixed and reported, and the
extrapolation split is defined purely by qubit count, so no large-circuit
information leaks into training.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

We recast barren-plateau screening for hardware-efficient circuits as
supervised prediction from architecture. Relative to the original preprint,
this revision changes the label to the mean gradient variance over all
parameters with structural zeros removed, evaluates only on architectures the
model has never seen, and measures the learned model against controls that
encode the known theory. The corrected numbers are, perhaps surprisingly, higher than those first
reported: the structural zeros of the original label were the main source of
unexplained variance, and once they are separated from genuine small
gradients the architecture determines the label almost completely. These are
the numbers we stand behind. A gradient-boosted model
predicts $\log_{10}$ of the mean gradient variance with $R^2=\RsqCV$ on
unseen architectures, against \RsqPhys{} for a physics-informed linear
model, and transfers to 11- and 12-qubit circuits with $R^2=\RsqExtrap$
(\RsqExtrapMLP{} for a neural network, which extrapolates the decay in $n$
where trees cannot, and \LargeRsqMLP{} on 13- and 14-qubit circuits when
trained on everything up to 12). Cost locality and entanglement structure carry most of
the signal, and transfer to an entanglement pattern never seen in training
is possible but lossy. The model is a
cheap screening tool for one circuit family; extending it to other families,
to noisy simulation, and to larger qubit counts via tensor-network labels are
natural next steps.
